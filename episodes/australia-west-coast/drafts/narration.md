# Why Australia's West Coast Has Only One City — narration draft (sourced: research.md)

## Hook (curious)

P0: Western Australia has nearly thirteen thousand kilometres of mainland coast. Fly along it at night, from the south-west corner to the Kimberley, and almost all of it is dark.

P1: There is one exception. Perth. About eight in every ten Western Australians live in and around that single city. Leave it behind and you can drive for days along the coast and pass only small towns, mining ports and empty beaches.

P2: But here is the strange part. The other side of Australia is lined with cities. And Carnarvon, on the west coast, sits almost as far from the equator as Brisbane does on the east. Brisbane gets about a thousand millimetres of rain in an average year. Carnarvon gets about two hundred and thirty.

P3: Same continent. Nearly the same latitude. More than four times the rain on one side. And now even Perth, the one city that made it, is losing the rain it was built on.

P4: So why does Australia's west coast have only one city? The answer runs through a wind that blows the wrong way, a river that hides underground, a Dutch shipwreck and a massacre, the strongest winds ever measured on the mainland, a town that was wiped off the map, and a city that now makes its own water from the sea. The first reason is the air itself.

## 1. The Wrong Side of the Wind (calm)

P5: Reason one. The west coast sits on the wrong side of the wind. Australia lies under a belt of high pressure that circles the globe at about thirty degrees south. Air in that belt sinks, warms and dries out. It is the same belt that makes the Atacama in Chile and the Namib in Africa, two deserts that also sit on the western edge of a continent.

P6: Around that high pressure, the winds blow from the south-east. On the east coast, those winds come in off the Pacific, loaded with moisture, and hit a long line of mountains. The air rises, cools and drops its rain. That is why Brisbane is green.

P7: By the time the same air reaches the west coast, it has crossed thousands of kilometres of dry land. It arrives from the land, not the sea. And there are no high mountains on this side to squeeze out what little moisture is left. The highest peak in the whole state, Mount Meharry, reaches only about twelve hundred and fifty metres, and it sits far inland.

P8: The ocean should help. Off most desert coasts, like Chile and Namibia, cold water wells up from the deep and keeps the air cool and dry. Off Western Australia, something unusual happens. A warm current, the Leeuwin Current, flows south from the tropics, along the coast. Scientists describe it as the only current of its kind in the world, an eastern boundary current that carries tropical heat towards the pole.

P9: The water off this coast is four to six degrees warmer than off other desert coasts at the same latitude. That warmth gives Perth and the south-west their mild, wetter winters. It feeds coral reefs far further south than usual. But it cannot fight the wind. North of Perth, the warm sea sits beside a dry shore.

## 2. Rivers That Hide (curious)

P10: Reason two. Without rain, there are no great rivers. The east coast has rivers that run all year. Most rivers in the west run only after a storm, and then vanish.

P11: The Gascoyne, which reaches the sea at Carnarvon, is the longest river in Western Australia. For most of the year its bed is dry sand. Locals call it the upside-down river, because its water is still there, flowing underneath, in the sand and gravel below the dry bed.

P12: That hidden water is the only fresh water Carnarvon has. Since about the nineteen twenties, farmers have pumped it to grow bananas, tomatoes and other crops, and Carnarvon became a major supplier of fresh fruit and vegetables for Perth. In the nineteen sixties, so many wells pulled so much water that the groundwater turned salty, and many of the plantations failed. Since then the state has limited how much anyone can pump.

P12b: Shark Bay, just south of Carnarvon, shows how harsh this water can be. In a shallow arm of the bay called Hamelin Pool, the sea is about twice as salty as normal. Almost nothing can live in it. So on its floor, mats of microbes build rocky mounds called stromatolites, the same kind of structures that fossils show on Earth billions of years ago. They survive here because nothing else does. Shark Bay became a World Heritage area in nineteen ninety-one.

P13: One small town, living on one hidden river, under strict limits. That is roughly the most this land can carry without help. And for a long time, the outsiders who saw it decided it could not carry anything at all.

## 3. The Ships That Kept Going (tension)

P14: Reason three. Europeans found this coast early, and they did not like what they found. The first Europeans known to have landed on the west coast were Dutch. Their ships sailed from the Cape of Good Hope on the strong westerly winds of the Southern Ocean, then turned north towards the spice islands of the East Indies. Turn too late, and you hit Australia.

P15: In October sixteen sixteen, a Dutch captain named Dirk Hartog did exactly that. He landed on an island at the edge of Shark Bay, nailed a flattened pewter plate to a post, and scratched a record of his visit onto it. It is the oldest European object known from Australia. Then he sailed on. There was no water, no trade and no reason to stay.

P15b: Eighty years later, another Dutch captain, Willem de Vlamingh, came looking for a lost ship. On the same island he found Hartog's plate, its post almost rotted away. He put up a new plate in its place and took the old one with him. It ended up in Amsterdam, where it is still on display in the Rijksmuseum. Two visits, eighty years apart, and in between no European had settled anywhere on this coast.

P16: Thirteen years later, the coast showed what it could do to a ship. On the fourth of June sixteen twenty-nine, the Batavia, one of the Dutch East India Company's newest ships, struck a reef in the Houtman Abrolhos, a chain of low islands off today's Geraldton. Most of the people aboard made it onto bare coral islands with almost no fresh water.

P17: The commander, Francisco Pelsaert, took a small boat north to the Dutch base in Java for help. While he was gone, a group of mutineers led by a company merchant named Jeronimus Cornelisz took control of the survivors. According to the Western Australian Museum, they killed about a hundred and twenty-five men, women and children. When Pelsaert returned with a rescue ship, the ringleaders were tried and executed on the islands. Almost four hundred years later, archaeologists digging on Beacon Island, where most of the killings happened, are still finding the graves of the victims. Two young mutineers were put ashore on the mainland instead. What happened to them is unknown. The wreck itself was found again in nineteen sixty-three, and part of its hull now stands in the Shipwreck Galleries in Fremantle, a short drive from Perth.

P18: The English came next. In sixteen eighty-eight, William Dampier landed on the north-west coast. In the account he published in sixteen ninety-seven, he described the soil as dry and sandy, with no water unless you dug wells. His bleak picture of the land stayed the common view for a long time.

P19: For more than two hundred years after Hartog, Europeans mapped this coast, wrecked ships on it, and sailed past it. Nobody stayed. When Britain finally did settle Australia, it chose the other side.

## 4. A Colony on Sand (calm)

P20: Reason four. When settlers finally came to the west, the land fought them too. In May eighteen twenty-nine, the Swan River Colony was founded where Perth stands today. Its founders had expected rich farmland.

P21: The soil turned out to be mostly sand. The good farmland was a narrow strip along the banks of the Swan and Canning rivers, and officials took most of it first. Gardens failed. Early settlements along the coast were abandoned. Twenty-one years after it was founded, the colony still had fewer than six thousand settlers.

P22: What changed things was gold, found far inland in the eighteen nineties. Thousands of miners rushed to Kalgoorlie and Coolgardie, into country with almost no water at all. So the colony did something huge. Its chief engineer, Charles Yelverton O'Connor, planned a steel pipeline to pump water from a dam in the hills near Perth all the way to the goldfields: five hundred and sixty-six kilometres, uphill, with eight pumping stations.

P23: The water arrived in January nineteen-oh-three. O'Connor did not see it. He had taken his own life ten months earlier, after fierce attacks on him and the project in the press. His pipeline still runs today.

P24: That pipeline shows the pattern of the whole state. Wealth was found far from the coast. And the water to keep people there had to come from Perth. The city became the place everything else depended on.

P24b: And the state kept pulling towards it. In nineteen eleven, about one in three Western Australians lived in Perth. By nineteen forty-seven it was one in two. Today it is eight in ten. Every decade, a larger share of the state moved to the one corner where the rain was reliable.

## 5. The Cyclone Coast (tension)

P25: Reason five. North of Perth, the coast is not just dry. It is dangerous. According to the Bureau of Meteorology, the north-west coast between Broome and Exmouth is Australia's most cyclone-prone stretch of coastline. Tropical cyclones cross it more often than anywhere else on the continent, mostly between December and April.

P26: In March nineteen ninety-nine, Severe Tropical Cyclone Vance passed close to Exmouth. Just before midday on the twenty-second, the weather office at Learmonth, thirty-five kilometres to the south, recorded a gust of two hundred and sixty-seven kilometres an hour. The Bureau listed it as a record for the Australian mainland.

P27: Inland, it is the heat. On the Bureau's original records, the Pilbara town of Marble Bar reached at least thirty-seven point eight degrees, a hundred degrees Fahrenheit, on one hundred and sixty days in a row, from the end of October nineteen twenty-three to April nineteen twenty-four.

P27b: The sea itself has killed whole fleets here. In the early twentieth century, Broome, on the north-west coast, was a pearling town, and hundreds of small sailing boats called luggers worked the shallow banks offshore, many crewed by Japanese and Malay divers. In March nineteen thirty-five, a cyclone passed north of the town and caught the fleet at sea near the Lacepede Islands. Twenty-one luggers were lost. About a hundred and forty men drowned.

P28: Put those together, a dry coast, cyclones in summer and long runs of extreme heat, and you get a place where building a town is expensive and keeping people in it is harder. Yet some of the richest ground on Earth lies right here.

## 6. Rich, But No Cities (curious)

P29: Reason six. The west coast is rich, but its riches do not need cities. Inland from the cyclone coast lies the Pilbara, with some of the largest iron ore deposits in the world. In the year to June twenty twenty-five, the ports of the Pilbara shipped about seven hundred and thirty million tonnes of iron ore. The state says that is around forty-three percent of the world's seaborne trade in it, and about four fifths of Australia's.

P30: Most of it leaves through one port. Port Hedland shipped almost five hundred and seventy-eight million tonnes, more than any other bulk export port in Australia. Yet the town around it, including South Hedland, had only about fifteen thousand people at the last census.

P31: The reason is simple. Mines here run on huge machines and long trains, not on large crowds of workers. Many workers do not live in the Pilbara at all. They fly in from Perth for a couple of weeks, work long shifts, and fly home. The money is made in the north. The families, the schools and the shops stay in Perth.

P32: And sometimes a mining town does not just stay small. It disappears. In the gorges of the Pilbara, a town called Wittenoom grew up around a blue asbestos mine. The company closed the mine at the end of nineteen sixty-six, and the government later found the town itself was contaminated.

P33: The state began winding Wittenoom down in nineteen seventy-eight. In two thousand and six it cut off the power. In two thousand and seven, the town was officially removed from the map. More than two thousand of the roughly twenty thousand people who worked or lived there have since died of asbestos diseases, according to health groups and the state. The last resident left in twenty twenty-two. About three million tonnes of mine waste still lie in the gorge, and the road signs warn visitors to stay away.

## 7. The One City Runs Dry (tension)

P34: Reason seven. Even the one city is under pressure, and from the same thing that emptied the rest of the coast: rain. Perth was built on wet winters, carried in from the Indian Ocean by storms from the south-west. The south-west corner is the only part of the coast where those winter storms reliably reach land, and that is exactly where the city grew. Its water came from dams in the hills east of the city.

P35: Since the middle of the nineteen seventies, those winter storms have weakened. According to Perth's water utility, the Water Corporation, rainfall in the dam catchments has dropped by fifteen to twenty percent. But the flow of water into the dams has fallen by about eighty percent, because dry ground soaks up most of the rain before it can run off.

P36: Before nineteen seventy-five, the dams received about four hundred and twenty billion litres a year. In the last decade, the average has been about seventy-five billion. This year, after a dry winter, the utility says little more than twelve and a half billion litres flowed in.

P37: So Perth turned to the sea. It opened its first large desalination plant in two thousand and six, and a second one south of the city later. Together, the plants can now supply about half of Perth's drinking water. The southern plant alone provides, on average, about thirty percent. A third plant, at Alkimos, north of the city, is due to add about fifty billion litres a year from twenty twenty-eight.

P37b: Perth has also been drawing down the water under its feet. In parts of the Gnangara Mound, the great body of groundwater north of the city, levels have fallen by up to ten metres since nineteen eighty. The state estimates about a thousand billion litres of stored water have been lost. So Perth now cleans its own wastewater to drinking standard and pumps it back underground, up to twenty-eight billion litres a year, to refill the aquifers for later.

P38: Think about what that means. The one city on Australia's west coast now makes a large share of its water from the ocean. The coast's only success story is fighting the same dry wind that kept the rest of the coast empty.

## Ending (calm)

P39: So why does the west coast have only one city? The wind comes from the land, not the sea. The rivers hide underground. The first Europeans who saw this coast sailed on, or wrecked on it. The soil was sand. The north is a cyclone coast. And the wealth that was found could be dug up and shipped out without building towns to hold it.

P40: Perth survived because it had the one corner of mild, wet winters, and then because everything else in the state came to depend on it. Now even that corner is drying.

P41: Some Australians argue the country should build up the north and west, with new ports, new towns and new water. Others say the coast is empty for good reasons, and that the money is better spent where people already live.

P41b: Whichever way that argument goes, the west coast will keep its shape for a long time. The wind will still come off the land. The rivers will still hide. And the one city on that long, dark coast will keep doing what it has done for almost two hundred years: finding water where the sky will not give it.

P42: Should Australia try to build a second big city on its west coast, or leave it as it is? Tell me what you think.
