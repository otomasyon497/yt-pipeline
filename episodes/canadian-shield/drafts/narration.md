# Why Almost Nobody Lives on Half of Canada — narration draft (sourced: research.md)

## Hook (curious)

P0: Canada is the second largest country on Earth. Look at it from space at night and almost all of it is dark. And the darkest part is a huge horseshoe of rock wrapped around Hudson Bay, from the Arctic down to the Great Lakes and out to Labrador.

P1: Geologists call it the Canadian Shield. It covers about five million square kilometres, roughly half of the country. We counted the people who live on its forest belt, using a population map built from satellite images and census data. About three point four million. Fewer than one Canadian in ten.

P2: But here is the strange part. On the other side of the Atlantic, Finland stands on the same kind of rock. Ancient, hard, scraped bare by the same kind of ice sheet, and covered in lakes. More than five and a half million people live there. Finland has a city of over two hundred thousand people close to the Arctic Circle. Canada's Shield has nothing like it that far north.

P3: So why does half of Canada stay almost empty? The answer runs through a frozen sea in the middle of the continent, a farming dream that ended in fire, a town whose houses were sold and hauled away, a railway that vanished for a year and a half, and a river of electricity that flows south. The first reason is under your feet.

## 1. Rock Without Soil (calm)

P4: Reason one. The Shield is the bare core of North America. In most places, the oldest rock of a continent is buried under kilometres of younger layers. Here, those layers are mostly gone. What you stand on near Sudbury or Thunder Bay is Precambrian rock, more than five hundred million years old, and in places some of the oldest rock ever found.

P5: Then came the ice. During the last ice age, an ice sheet kilometres thick covered almost all of Canada. As it crept outward, it froze rocks and gravel into its base and ground across the land like sandpaper. It scraped off the soil and pushed much of it south.

P6: When the ice melted, it left bare ridges, a thin layer of sand and boulders, and hollows that filled with water. That is why the Shield looks the way it does from a plane. Rock, black spruce, and lakes. Lakes beyond counting.

P7: But that is only half an answer. Finland went through the same thing. Its bedrock is part of the Fennoscandian Shield, just as old, and the ice scraped it too. The soil there is thin. The lakes are everywhere. So the rock cannot be the whole story. Something else must make the Canadian side so much harder.

## 2. The Freezer in the Middle (curious)

P8: Reason two. Canada's Shield has a frozen sea at its centre. Together with James Bay at its southern end, Hudson Bay covers more than a million square kilometres, deep inside the continent. For most of the year, it is covered in ice.

P9: Ships heading for the port of Churchill, on its western shore, could expect the ice to break up around the middle of July and to freeze again in the second half of November. That leaves about eight months of ice. When the ice melts, the polar bears come ashore and wait, fasting, for about four months until it returns.

P9b: In Churchill itself, the bears are part of daily life. Since 1983, the town has run a Polar Bear Alert program. Anyone who sees a bear calls a hotline, and conservation officers scare it away or trap it. Bears that keep coming back are held in a building on the edge of town, opened in 1982, until the bay freezes and they can be released onto the ice.

P10: Now compare two places at almost the same latitude. Churchill sits a little south of Stockholm and a little south of Helsinki. We pulled thirty years of daily temperatures, from 1991 to 2020, from a global weather record for each place.

P11: Helsinki averages about six degrees above zero over the year. Churchill averages six degrees below. In January, Helsinki sits around minus four. Churchill sits around minus twenty-six. Churchill spends more than two hundred days a year with a daily average below freezing. Helsinki spends fewer than a hundred.

P12: Go further south in Canada and it is still colder than you would expect. Timmins, a mining city in northern Ontario, lies at about the same latitude as Paris. Oulu, in Finland, lies near the Arctic Circle, about eighteen hundred kilometres further north. And yet, over a whole year, Oulu is slightly warmer than Timmins.

P13: Why? Part of it is the wind. Northern Europe gets its weather mostly from the west, off a warm Atlantic Ocean. Scientists still argue about how much of that warmth comes from ocean currents and how much from the winds themselves. But the result is the same. Finland's air comes off the sea.

P14: The Canadian Shield gets its weather off a continent, and off a bay that stays frozen for most of the year. The same rock, at the same latitude, sits in a completely different climate. And that difference decided what happened when people tried to farm it.

## 3. The Farm Dream That Burned (tension)

P15: Reason three. Canada tried to farm the Shield, and the Shield fought back. At the end of the ice age, a huge lake of meltwater sat against the ice in what is now northern Ontario and Quebec. When it drained, it left behind a wide, flat floor of clay. It became known as the Great Clay Belt.

P16: To officials in Toronto, it looked like a second Ontario. A geologist named Alfred Barlow praised the clay in a report in 1899. The next year, the province sent ten survey parties into the north. Pamphlets promised the great Clay Belt of northern Ontario, containing over fifteen million acres.

P17: Settlers came by the new railways. They cut the forest and burned the brush to clear their fields. In the summer of 1916, there was little rain. On the twenty-ninth of July, small clearing fires around the settlements joined together into one.

P18: The fire swept through towns and farms along the railway. Matheson was almost destroyed. In the hamlet of Nushka, people ran from the flames, and many never got out. The official count was about two hundred and twenty-three dead, and the real number is unknown. It is often called the deadliest fire in Canadian history.

P19: Six years later, it happened again. On the fourth of October, 1922, fires in the smaller clay belt near Lake Timiskaming were driven by strong winds. Within hours, about ninety percent of the town of Haileybury was gone. Around forty-three people died and about three thousand five hundred lost their homes. The fire stopped only when the wind dropped and snow and rain arrived.

P20: Even after the fires, the government kept trying. After the First World War, it set up a farm colony at Kapuskasing, in the heart of the Clay Belt, for soldiers coming home from Europe. The scheme was dropped in 1920, and many of its farmers left for the cities or for the prairies.

P20b: The fires were not the only problem. The clay was fertile, but the winters were long and snowy, and the rain came at the wrong times in a short growing season. Most farms gave little. The Clay Belt became logging country, with paper mills where the wheat was supposed to grow. And there was another reason the settlers did not have to stay.

## 4. Somewhere Better to Go (calm)

P21: Reason four. Canada had better land, and Finland did not. Look at the map again. South of the Shield lies a narrow strip of younger, flatter rock, from Windsor through Toronto and Ottawa to Montreal and Quebec City. The soil the ice scraped off the north ended up partly here.

P22: We measured how many people live in that strip, the band of mixed forest and farmland along the Great Lakes and the St Lawrence. It covers only about two percent of Canada's land. More than half of all Canadians live in it.

P23: And to the west lay the prairies. Under the Dominion Lands Act of 1872, a settler could claim a quarter section, a hundred and sixty acres of flat, deep soil, for a ten dollar fee, and farm it. Much of the prairies was settled this way, and the railways carried the settlers straight across the Shield to reach them. For a family looking for land, the choice was easy. Why fight the rock when you could ride past it?

P24: Finland never had that choice. Almost the whole country stands on shield rock. People cleared fields wherever they could, built their towns along the coasts, and made the best of what they had, because there was nowhere else to go. Canada did have somewhere else, so the Shield was left for the people who needed what was under it.

P24b: And look where the Shield's few cities stand. The largest are Greater Sudbury, with about a hundred and seventy thousand people, Saguenay, with about a hundred and sixty thousand, and Thunder Bay, with about a hundred and twenty thousand. All three sit close to its southern edge, on a railway, a lake or a river that leads to the St Lawrence. Not one city of that size has grown in its interior.

## 5. Towns That Live as Long as the Mine (tension)

P25: Reason five. On the Shield, a town lives as long as its mine. The rock that is so bad for farming is rich in iron, nickel, gold, copper, zinc and uranium. So towns grew where the ore was, not where people wanted to live. And when the ore ran out, or the price fell, the town had no reason left.

P26: In the early nineteen fifties, the Iron Ore Company of Canada built a town in the far north of Quebec, near the Labrador border. They called it Schefferville. A railway hundreds of kilometres long carried the ore south to the coast. By its peak, around five thousand people lived there.

P27: On the second of November, 1982, the company announced that the mine would close. Mining stopped the next year. Families left, and buildings were pulled down. In 2021, the census counted two hundred and forty-four people in Schefferville.

P28: Pine Point, on the south shore of Great Slave Lake, went even further. It was a zinc and lead mining town with a school, an arena and nearly two thousand people in 1976. The mine closed in 1987. The town was closed the next year.

P29: Its houses were sold cheaply and moved away, many of them to Hay River and Fort Resolution. Other buildings were knocked down. By 1991, nine people were left. From above, you can still see the streets, laid out in loops in the forest, leading to houses that are no longer there.

P30: These towns were never failures. They did exactly what they were built for. But a town built only for a mine cannot grow into a city, and the Shield is full of towns like that. Which brings us to the next problem. Even the towns that survive have to be reached.

## 6. One Line to the Sea (tension)

P31: Reason six. On the Shield, every road and railway is a fight, and many places have only one. Churchill, on Hudson Bay, has no road to the rest of Canada at all. For almost a century, its link to the south has been a single railway line.

P32: That line reached Churchill on the twenty-ninth of March, 1929. A crew of about three thousand men had laid track with picks and wheelbarrows across frozen muskeg, the boggy ground that covers much of the land around the bay. The port was finished two years later, to ship prairie grain to Europe through the bay.

P33: In May 2017, record floods washed out the track in about twenty places along roughly four hundred kilometres. The owner of the line said it would not pay for the repairs without government help. The trains stopped. Food and fuel had to be flown in.

P34: For eighteen months, Churchill was cut off from the rest of the country, except by air. The line reopened on the first of November, 2018, after a deal that brought new owners, and the federal government put in one hundred and seventeen million dollars to buy and repair it. When the first passenger train arrived that December, people came out to meet it.

P35: That is what living on the Shield means. Not just cold and rock, but distance, and the one thin line that crosses it. Yet the most important lines on the Shield do not carry people at all. They carry electricity.

## 7. The Empty Half Lights the Full Half (curious)

P36: Reason seven. The empty half of Canada powers the full half. The ice that scraped the Shield bare also left it covered in rivers and lakes, dropping from the high ground towards Hudson Bay and the Atlantic. Canada is the third largest producer of hydroelectricity in the world, and more than half of it is made in Quebec.

P37: On the thirtieth of April, 1971, the Premier of Quebec, Robert Bourassa, announced what he called the project of the century. Huge dams on the rivers that run into James Bay, the southern arm of Hudson Bay. The Cree and Inuit who lived there had not been asked. They went to court and won an injunction to stop the work, but it was soon overturned.

P38: In November 1975, after years of talks, the Cree, the Inuit, Quebec, Canada and Hydro-Québec signed the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement. The Cree and Inuit gave up claims to much of the land in return for two hundred and twenty-five million dollars and rights over hunting, fishing and their own communities. It is known as Canada's first modern treaty.

P39: Today, the La Grande complex has about seventeen thousand megawatts of generating capacity, close to half of everything Hydro-Québec can produce. The largest station alone, Robert-Bourassa, can deliver more than five and a half thousand megawatts. The power travels about a thousand kilometres south, to Montreal and beyond.

P39b: The dams changed the land they were built on. In September 1984, thousands of caribou of the George River herd drowned while crossing the Caniapiscau River at Limestone Falls, on a river the project had changed. Quebec officials first estimated at least ten thousand. Inuit leaders blamed water released from the new reservoir upstream. Hydro-Québec said heavy rain had raised the river. Fences were later built near the falls to keep the herds away from the worst stretch.

P39c: Flooding the forest caused a slower problem. When land goes under water, bacteria in the drowned plants turn natural mercury into a form that builds up in fish. Hydro-Québec's own monitoring found that mercury in fish rose to between two and eight times its normal level after the reservoirs were filled, and took twenty to thirty years to come back down in the fish that eat other fish. The Cree, who fish these waters, were given advice on which fish were safe to eat.

P40: In Labrador, there is another story. In 1969, Newfoundland signed a contract to sell power from the Churchill Falls station to Hydro-Québec until 2041, at a fixed price. That price was about a fifth of a cent for each kilowatt-hour. For decades, Hydro-Québec earned far more from that power than Newfoundland did, and the contract became a lasting grievance in the province.

P41: In 2026, the two provinces agreed a new deal. Under it, the price starts at one point eight cents in 2027 and rises from there. But the pattern stays the same. The power is made on the Shield, where almost nobody lives, and used in the south, where almost everybody does.

## Ending (calm)

P42: So why does almost nobody live on half of Canada? The ice took the soil. A frozen sea in the middle makes it far colder than Finland at the same latitude. The farms burned and failed. The country had better land to the south and west. Its towns live and die with their mines. Every place is a long way from the next, often joined by a single road or a single railway. And its greatest wealth, the rock and the falling water, can be taken south without anyone having to stay.

P43: Finland made a home on its shield because it had to. Canada never had to. Would you move to a town on the Shield, with its lakes and its winters, if the job paid twice as much?
