# Why This American Highway Dives Under the Sea — narration draft (sourced: research.md)

## Hook (curious)
P0: You leave the shore of Virginia Beach and the road runs straight out to sea. Low concrete, a few feet of railing, and water on both sides. After a few miles the land behind you is a thin line. Ahead, the highway runs onto a tiny island, about five acres of rock and sand in the middle of the bay. And then the road tips down and disappears into the water.

P1: This is the Chesapeake Bay Bridge-Tunnel. Seventeen point six miles from shore to shore, across the mouth of the largest estuary in the United States. Twice on the way across, it dives under the sea, and while you drive through the tunnel, a cargo ship or a navy destroyer can pass over your head.

P2: Most people ask how they built it. The better question is why it had to be built like this at all. Why is there seventeen miles of open water here, with two deep channels cut through it? Why would anyone pay to send a highway under the sea, when a bridge would have been cheaper? The answers go back to a rock from space, an ice age, and the largest naval base in the world. And there is one more thing, which we will come to at the end. There is a tide gauge on this crossing. Since nineteen seventy-five, the sea it measures has risen by about a foot.

## 1. A Hole Older Than the Bay (calm)
P3: About thirty-five million years ago, there was no Chesapeake Bay. The coast of North America ran much farther inland, roughly where Richmond is today, and everything east of it was shallow sea. Then, according to the U.S. Geological Survey, an asteroid or comet a few miles wide came down into that sea, right about where the mouth of the bay is now.

P4: The impact blasted a crater that is among the twenty largest known on Earth. Glassy drops of melted rock rained down as far away as Texas and the Caribbean. Tsunamis spread across most of the North Atlantic. And then, over millions of years, the hole slowly filled in. Today the crater lies buried under a thousand to fifteen hundred feet of sand, mud and rock. You cannot see it from the road. But it never really went away.

P5: Its rim is still there under the ground, and the rubble inside it kept settling for millions of years, so the land above it stayed a little lower than the land around it. The National Park Service puts it simply: this large depression helped determine where the Chesapeake Bay would form, millions of years later. The rock from space did not dig the bay. It left a low spot, and the rivers found it.

P6: The bay itself is young. About twenty thousand years ago, at the height of the last ice age, so much water was locked up in ice sheets that the sea stood far below where it is now. The shore lay far to the east, out on what is now the seafloor. Where the bay is now, there was a river valley. The Susquehanna River ran down it, joined by the Potomac, the Rappahannock, the York and the James, and flowed out across dry land to a distant shore.

P7: Then the ice melted, and the ocean came back. By about ten thousand years ago, the rising sea had flooded the lower end of that valley. The water kept climbing up the old river and its branches, until about three thousand years ago the bay reached roughly the shape it has today. A drowned river system, opening to the Atlantic through one wide gap.

P8: That gap is where the bridge-tunnel crosses. And the drowned river did not disappear. The Geological Survey notes that remnants of the old Susquehanna still run along the bottom of the bay as a deep channel. Big ships need deep water. That is the first part of the answer. The road had to cross a seventeen-mile mouth, and it had to cross the deep paths that ships still follow. Which raises the next question: who needed to cross it so badly?

## 2. Cut Off (calm)
P9: Look at the map again. North of the bay's mouth is a long, flat peninsula, shared by Delaware, Maryland and Virginia. Its southern tip is the Eastern Shore of Virginia, two counties of farms, fishing villages and marsh. To reach the rest of Virginia, the people who lived there had a choice. Drive north and all the way around the bay, a trip of hours. Or take a boat.

P10: For most of the twentieth century, the boat was a ferry. By the late years of the service, the crossing ran from Little Creek near Norfolk to Kiptopeke, near Cape Charles. A ferry could carry about sixty cars and took about ninety minutes for the eighteen-mile trip. In fog or a hard wind, the boats waited, and so did everyone on them.

P11: In nineteen fifty-four, Virginia created a public commission to take over the ferries, and soon it was asked a bigger question. Could the mouth of the bay be crossed by road? A few miles to the west, the answer was already being built. In nineteen fifty-seven, the Hampton Roads Bridge-Tunnel opened, a three and a half mile crossing that ran out on trestles and then dived under the channel into Norfolk harbor. It proved a road could go under a shipping lane. The Chesapeake crossing would have to do it twice, on a crossing five times as long.

P12: But why not simply build a very tall bridge? Engineers built tall bridges all over the country in the nineteen fifties. Here, though, someone else had a say. And they were the most powerful neighbor on the bay.

## 3. The Navy's Veto (tension)
P13: Just inside the mouth of the bay is Hampton Roads, one of the great natural harbors of the world, where the James and the Elizabeth rivers meet the bay. And on its southern shore, on the grounds of a nineteen-oh-seven world's fair, the United States Navy built a base in nineteen seventeen. It grew into Naval Station Norfolk, which the Navy describes as the world's largest naval installation, home port of aircraft carriers, destroyers and dozens of other ships.

P14: Every one of those ships reaches the Atlantic the same way. Through the deep, dredged Thimble Shoal Channel, straight across the line where the crossing would have to go. In the middle of the Cold War, that made a bridge a problem. According to histories of the project, the Navy objected to any bridge over the channel. If a bridge collapsed, by accident, in a storm or through sabotage, the wreckage could close the channel and trap the fleet inside the bay.

P15: There was a second channel too, farther north, the Chesapeake Channel, which carried ships up the bay to Baltimore. Maryland officials wanted that one kept clear as well. So the plan became a chain. Long, low trestles across the shallow water. Two higher bridges over smaller channels. And at the two places where the big ships pass, the road would leave the surface and go under them.

P16: But a tunnel needs a place to start. There is no land at the edge of a shipping channel in the middle of a bay, only open water. So before anyone could build a tunnel, they had to build the ground it would start from. Four islands, in the open sea.

## 4. Building Land in the Sea (calm)
P17: First, the money. In nineteen sixty, the commission sold two hundred million dollars in bonds to private investors, to be paid back by the tolls drivers would pay. According to the crossing's own history, no local, state or federal tax money went into the construction. If the road failed to earn its tolls, the investors would lose, not the taxpayers.

P18: Work began in the autumn of nineteen sixty. To make each island, crews first built rings of rock on the bottom of the bay, then pumped sand into the middle until it rose above the water. Then they covered the outside with armor. Granite boulders of two to four tons each, brought from a quarry in central Virginia. Each island ended up about five and a quarter acres, roughly the size of four football fields.

P19: The tunnels were not dug. They were built somewhere else and sunk into place. Steel tubes, each about three hundred feet long and thirty-five feet across, were made at a shipyard in Orange, Texas, sealed at the ends so they would float, and towed all the way around Florida to Virginia. Thirty-seven of them for each tunnel. On the way, two broke away from their tugs in the Gulf of Mexico, and both were caught again.

P20: At Norfolk the tubes got a thick shell of concrete. Then, one by one, they were sunk into a trench dug across the bottom of each channel, joined underwater, pumped dry, and buried. When it was done, ships could pass over the tunnels with no idea that a highway lay underneath.

P21: The trestles were a different problem. There is no bedrock to stand on here. Under the bay is the fill of that old crater and the sediments that came after it, and solid rock lies more than six thousand feet down. So the concrete piles hold the road up by friction alone, gripping the mud. The longest was driven a hundred and seventy-two feet deep.

P22: Then, in March of nineteen sixty-two, the Atlantic tested the project. A nor'easter stalled off the coast for days, the storm later called the Ash Wednesday Storm. Tides ran high through Norfolk, waves broke over the work sites, and according to reporting from the time, the storm stripped away great amounts of fill and sand, knocked over a tunnel section, and wrecked the crew's main pile driver, a giant platform called the Big D.

P23: The work went on. On April fifteenth, nineteen sixty-four, forty-two months after construction began, the Chesapeake Bay Bridge-Tunnel opened. The ferries were retired. The next year, a competition among more than a hundred major projects named it one of the Seven Engineering Wonders of the Modern World. Accounts of the project say seven workers died building it.

P24: For the first time, the Eastern Shore was joined to the rest of Virginia by road. But the bay had not agreed to anything. And it was about to make that clear.

## 5. The Bay Pushes Back (tension)
P25: The trestles stand in open water, and the ships that wait to enter Hampton Roads anchor all around them. In the night of January twenty-first, nineteen seventy, a gale came up. According to a later account from the Hampton Roads Naval Museum, gusts reached thirty-five to fifty knots, and six other ships dragged their anchors that night. One of them was the Navy cargo ship Yancey.

P26: The Yancey's anchor would not hold. The wind pushed her across the bay, stern first, and at one thirty-seven in the morning she hit the trestle. At three thirty she hit it again, and went through. She left a gap of about four hundred feet in the road. The crossing had stockpiled spare seventy-five-foot spans, and that helped, but in the cold and the heavy seas it was still about six weeks before the road reopened. It was not the first ship to strike the trestle, and it would not be the last.

P27: Then there is the wind itself. The crossing runs across open water, and nothing breaks the wind coming off the Atlantic. So it has rules you will find on very few roads. At thirty-five miles an hour of wind, the speed limit drops to forty-five and passing is banned. At forty, motorcycles, campers, anything being towed, box trucks, buses and empty tractor-trailers are turned away. At fifty, only cars, empty pickups, minivans and SUVs may cross. At sixty, the crossing closes.

P28: Even so, vehicles have gone over the side more than a dozen times since the nineteen eighties, most of them trucks. By one count, at least twenty people have died in them. Some crashes came in high wind. Others came on calm days, when a driver swerved, a tire blew, or a truck simply drifted.

P29: For thirty-five years, the whole crossing was one two-lane road, one lane each way, with nothing in between. In nineteen ninety-nine, a second, parallel trestle opened, and each direction got its own bridge. But not its own tunnel. The new tunnels were left out to save money. So even today, when you reach an island, four lanes squeeze into two, and you drive under the sea in a single tube, with traffic coming toward you a few feet away.

## 6. Chessie and the Anchor (curious)
P30: The fix is a second tunnel. In twenty sixteen, the crossing signed a contract worth about seven hundred and fifty-seven million dollars for a new tube under Thimble Shoal Channel. This time it would not be sunk into a trench across a channel used by the Navy. It would be bored underneath it, by a German-built tunnel boring machine forty-three feet across and three hundred and eight feet long. Its name, chosen by a schoolgirl in a public contest, was Chessie.

P31: Chessie started mining in February twenty twenty-three. In May, it hit something it could not cut. It turned out to be a large ship's anchor, buried in the mud of the channel, made in England around the turn of the twentieth century. Someone had lost it there, more than a hundred years ago, at the mouth of a busy harbor.

P32: Removing it, deep under the channel, safely, took eight more months of work. Tunnelling only resumed in May twenty twenty-four, at about fifty feet a day. On January twenty-seventh, twenty twenty-five, after more than six thousand three hundred feet, Chessie broke through into its receiving pit on the second island. The crossing's current forecast for opening the new tunnel is early twenty twenty-eight.

P33: And that is only one of the two tunnels. The second, under the Chesapeake Channel, is still decades away. So the bay keeps doing what it has done for millions of years. It slows things down. Which brings us back to the number from the beginning.

## 7. The Sinking Ground (tension)
P34: Across the water from the first island is a tide gauge at Sewells Point, on the Navy base in Norfolk. It has measured the sea there since nineteen twenty-seven, almost a hundred years. Over that time, according to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, the average sea level has risen at about one point nine inches every ten years. That adds up to about a foot and a half since the gauge went in.

P35: We compared that with every tide gauge NOAA lists on the Atlantic coast of the United States whose record goes back to the nineteen thirties or earlier. There are twenty-three. Sewells Point has the fastest rise of all of them. Faster than New York, faster than Boston, almost twice as fast as Key West. And the gauge on the bridge-tunnel itself, out in the channel, has measured about two point four inches per decade since nineteen seventy-five. About a foot in fifty years.

P36: Part of that is the ocean rising everywhere. But here the land is also going down. The U.S. Geological Survey says coastal Virginia has the highest rates of relative sea level rise on the entire Atlantic coast, because the ground is sinking at the same time. Much of it comes from pumping. For decades, towns and industry have drawn water from deep aquifers, and as the water comes out, the clay layers squeeze together and the land settles. Part of it is the slow sag of the crust as it adjusts to the vanished ice sheets of the north. And researchers studying the sinking around Hampton Roads list one more cause among the others: ongoing shifts in the buried crater.

P37: The crater is still there in other ways too. Deep under the bay, the rock is soaked in water that is about twice as salty as today's ocean. In twenty thirteen, USGS scientists showed that it is seawater from the Early Cretaceous, the time of the dinosaurs, a hundred to a hundred and forty-five million years old, trapped and kept in place partly by the shattered rock of the impact.

## Ending (calm)
P38: So this is why the highway dives under the sea. A rock from space left a low spot. An ice age carved a river valley through it, and the melting ice drowned it. The deepest parts of the drowned river became shipping channels. And a navy that needed those channels open made the road go underneath.

P39: The islands were built in the early nineteen sixties for the sea of the early nineteen sixties. Since then the water around them has risen by about a foot, and the ground under Norfolk is still sinking. The next tunnel under the Chesapeake Channel is planned for decades from now. So here is the question to leave in the comments: should it be built for the sea we have today, or for the sea of twenty one hundred?
