# Why Almost Nobody Lives on Florida's Big Bend Coast — narration draft (sourced: research.md, OK rows; CHECK rows attributed)

## Hook (curious)
P0: On the night Hurricane Helene came ashore in September 2024, a tide gauge at Cedar Key, Florida, measured the sea nine point three feet above the normal high-tide line. That gauge had been reporting since 1914. In all those years, it had never recorded water that high. And Cedar Key is not a city. It is a town of seven hundred and thirty-six people, on one of the emptiest stretches of coast in the eastern United States.

P1: Florida is home to about twenty-three point four million people, and much of it lives along the coast, in a long chain of beach towns. Pinellas County alone, around St. Petersburg, holds almost a million. But follow the same Gulf coast north, to the long curve where the peninsula turns into the Panhandle. This is the Big Bend. Four counties along that shore, Jefferson, Taylor, Dixie and Levy, hold about a hundred and three thousand people between them. Seen from space at night, this stretch of coast is almost dark.

P1b: The towns along it are tiny. Horseshoe Beach has a hundred and seventy-three people. St. Marks, three hundred and twelve. Yankeetown, six hundred and thirty-three. The biggest town behind this coast, Perry, has about seven thousand three hundred, and it sits well inland. There is no big city, no resort strip, and for most of its length, no beach at all.

P2: Over the next few minutes you'll see a seafloor so flat that three miles out from Cedar Key the water is barely deeper than a person is tall. A town that once hoped to be Florida's great Gulf port and lost it to a railroad built somewhere else. A canal across Florida that was stopped at twenty-eight percent. And why that same flat seafloor sent the water at Cedar Key higher than anyone had ever measured. So why does a state that lives on its coastline leave this one almost empty? It isn't one thing. It's five, and they all start in the same place. The first reason is under the water.

## 1. A Sea That Forgot to Get Deep (calm)
P3: Stand on almost any beach in Florida and walk into the sea, and the bottom drops away. Off Pensacola Beach, we measured the distance to the point where the water is ten metres deep, about thirty-three feet, using NOAA's global seafloor model. It's about a mile. Off Miami Beach, about a mile and a quarter. Off Panama City Beach, less than a third of a mile.

P4: Now do the same along the Big Bend. Off Keaton Beach, the ten-metre line is about thirteen miles out. Off Steinhatchee and St. Marks, about sixteen. Off Cedar Key, about fifteen. Off Crystal River, at the southern end, about twenty-two miles. Even Clearwater and Naples, on the same Gulf, get there in about seven and a half.

P5: Go three miles out from shore and the difference is even easier to picture. Off Cedar Key, our measurement puts the water at about five and a half feet deep. Off Keaton Beach, about eight. Off Pensacola, about seventy feet. Off Miami, about two hundred and seventy. And at thirty miles out from Cedar Key, the Gulf is still only about fifty feet deep. Thirty miles out from Pensacola, it is already more than three hundred. These numbers come from a model with cells about a mile across, so treat them as rough, but the pattern is not subtle.

P6: Geologists at the U.S. Geological Survey put a number on how gentle this slope is: about one in five thousand. That works out to roughly one foot of depth for every mile you travel out to sea. It is so flat, their report notes, that this coast has been described as an "incipient epicontinental sea": a place where the ocean is only just beginning to spread over the land.

P7: That flat floor explains the first strange thing about the Big Bend. It has almost no beaches. The same USGS work describes the marsh coast here as lower in wave energy than the sandy coast to the south, and the region as sediment-starved. The Gulf in front of it is small and sheltered, its waves are weak, and little sand ever reaches this shore. So instead of a beach, the land simply runs out into salt marsh, and the marsh runs out into the sea.

P8: And the shallow water isn't empty. It is carpeted with seagrass. The Big Bend Seagrasses Aquatic Preserve covers nine hundred and eighty-four thousand acres, the largest aquatic preserve in Florida, and one of the largest continuous seagrass meadows in the Gulf. Which raises a question we'll come back to at the end: is this coast a failure to develop, or one of the few places Florida never got around to ruining? Tell me in the comments which way you lean.

P9: For people who wanted to build here, though, that seafloor was a problem with no answer. A port needs deep water close to shore. Ships can't sail to a dock across fifteen miles of water you could almost wade. And there was a second problem, one you can't see from the sea at all, because it's underneath the land.

## 2. Ground Full of Holes (curious)
P10: Most of the land behind this coast sits on limestone, covered by a thin layer of sand. Rainwater soaks straight through the sand and slowly dissolves the rock underneath. Over thousands of years, that has turned the ground into something closer to a sponge: caves, sinkholes and underground channels. Geologists call this kind of landscape karst, and the Big Bend is full of it.

P11: The clearest proof is the springs. Wakulla Springs, south of Tallahassee, pours out about six hundred and twenty-three cubic feet of water every second on average, roughly four hundred million gallons a day, according to the Northwest Florida Water Management District. Its vent opens about a hundred and eighty feet below the surface into a huge network of flooded caves. And it's not unique. Florida has thirty-one springs that big.

P12: Some rivers here don't even stay on the surface. The Aucilla River, which reaches the Gulf in the middle of the Big Bend, disappears underground and comes back up again around thirty times along its lower course, according to a state water management plan, before it rises for the last time and runs out into the marsh.

P13: Look at where those rivers end up. There is no delta, no wide bay to shelter a harbour, no deep river mouth. The rivers fray out into dozens of small creeks and islands of marsh grass, and the shallow sea beyond them goes on for miles. Low land, soft wet ground with holes in it, and a sea you can't bring a ship across.

P14: So it's not surprising that some of the first big decisions made about this coast were not about building on it. In 1931, an executive order created what is now the St. Marks National Wildlife Refuge, as winter habitat for migratory birds. Today it covers more than eighty-six thousand acres and about forty-three miles of this coast. But seventy years before that refuge, people did try to build a city here. And for a while, it worked.

## 3. The Boom That Already Happened (tension)
P15: Out at the end of a chain of small islands lies Cedar Key. In the middle of the nineteenth century, this was the spot chosen to connect the Gulf to the rest of the country. Federal surveyors charted its waters in detail in 1852, and in 1861, according to historians of the line, the first train arrived from Fernandina on the Atlantic coast. Cedar Key became the Gulf end of Florida's first railroad across the peninsula.

P16: The town set out to become a port. According to the town's own history, pencil companies, including Eberhard Faber and the Eagle Pencil Company, built mills on the islands to cut the local red cedar into pencil slats and ship them north. Here is Cedar Key in 1884, drawn from above as a bird's-eye view: wharves, warehouses, a railroad running out over the water.

P17: But that same year, the town's future was being decided somewhere else. In 1883, the businessman Henry Plant bought control of the South Florida Railroad and pushed it all the way to Tampa Bay, with full service from 1884, according to the University of Florida's libraries. Tampa had what Cedar Key never really had: a big, sheltered bay. Historians of the town say much of Cedar Key's shipping followed Plant's tracks south. Census counts on record show Tampa growing from seven hundred and twenty people in 1880 to more than five thousand five hundred in 1890.

P18: Then came the night that finished the boom. On the twenty-ninth of September 1896, a hurricane came ashore near Cedar Key with winds of around a hundred and twenty-five miles an hour. According to the National Weather Service, it pushed the water ten and a half feet above sea level at Cedar Key and around twelve and a half feet at nearby Yankeetown. About a hundred people died, most of them drowned by the surge.

P19: Accounts of the town's history say the storm destroyed the Eagle mill, and that Faber's mill closed within two years, with the cedar forests already cut out. The town rebuilt on a neighbouring island, but it never became a port again. If you like stories where a map explains what history did, subscribing is how you'll find the next one. Because Cedar Key's collapse didn't end the plans for this coast. Over the next century, Florida tried again.

## 4. Every Plan After It (tension)
P20: The biggest plan was meant to cut Florida in half. The Cross Florida Barge Canal would link the Atlantic to the Gulf of Mexico, so ships could cross Florida instead of sailing all the way around it. Its western end was to reach the Gulf at the southern edge of the Big Bend.

P21: Work started in the 1930s, as a New Deal project, and stalled after about three years. In 1964 it started again under President Johnson. Parts of it were dug and built. Then, in 1971, President Nixon halted construction for good, citing the damage it would do to the environment. By then the canal was twenty-eight percent complete, and around seventy-five million dollars had been spent, according to the Florida Museum at the University of Florida.

P22: You can still see a piece of it from orbit. This is where the finished western section meets the Gulf. Look how far the dredged channel runs out to sea, marked by a line of spoil islands, before the water gets deep enough to matter. The rest of the route never became a canal. Today it's the Marjorie Harris Carr Cross Florida Greenway, more than seventy thousand acres of trails and forest across a hundred and ten miles of Florida.

P23: The Big Bend's other big bet was paper. Near Perry, the Foley pulp mill turned the pine forests behind the coast into cellulose, the biggest kind of employer a county this small can have. On the eighteenth of September 2023, Georgia-Pacific announced that it would permanently close the Foley mill, with about five hundred and twenty-five jobs lost. University of Florida economists estimated the total effect across the state at up to one thousand eight hundred and seventy-nine jobs.

P24: That announcement came about two weeks after Hurricane Idalia had come ashore about twenty miles away. So which was the bigger blow to this coast: the storms, or the jobs leaving? Have a think about that one. Because the storms were only getting started, and the reason they hit so hard here is the same flat seafloor we started with.

## 5. The Same Shelf, Turned Into a Weapon (tension)
P25: A storm surge is the rise of the sea caused by a storm itself, mostly by its winds pushing water toward land. NOAA lists what decides how high it climbs: the angle the storm comes in at, its strength, its size, its speed, and the shape of the seafloor. On the Big Bend, the wind has fifteen miles of shallow water to push before it reaches the coast.

P26: On the thirtieth of August 2023, Hurricane Idalia came ashore near Keaton Beach as a category three storm, with winds of about a hundred and fifteen miles an hour. The National Hurricane Center ranked it the third-strongest hurricane to affect the Big Bend, behind only the Cedar Key hurricane of 1896 and Hurricane Easy in 1950. The surge flooded land eight to twelve feet deep from Keaton Beach to Steinhatchee, and the Cedar Key gauge read six point eight nine feet above the high-tide line, its record at the time. Idalia caused an estimated three point six billion dollars of damage in the United States.

P27: Less than a year later, on the fifth of August 2024, Hurricane Debby made landfall just west of Steinhatchee, a category one storm, with surge flooding of up to six feet. And seven weeks after that came Helene.

P28: Helene had grown fast over the warm Gulf. Six hours before landfall, about eighty nautical miles off the coast, it reached its peak: winds of around a hundred and forty miles an hour. It came ashore about ten nautical miles southwest of Perry, late on the night of the twenty-sixth of September 2024, as a category four hurricane. The Hurricane Center calls it the strongest hurricane to make landfall in the Big Bend since reliable records began around 1900. From just west of Keaton Beach through Steinhatchee, the surge put twelve to sixteen feet of water over the land. South of there, through Horseshoe Beach and down to Cedar Key, eight to twelve feet.

P29: This is Keaton Beach in early September 2024, and this is the same coast a few weeks after Helene. The marsh behind the houses has turned brown. Further south, at Horseshoe Beach, you can see sand and mud washed across the point. On the ground, the day after landfall, the Air National Guard was clearing the roads through what was left of the houses.

P30: Fourteen people died in Helene's storm surge, all of them in Florida. The storm went on to kill at least a hundred and seventy-five people across the southeastern United States. Media reports cited by the Hurricane Center say about a quarter of the homes in Cedar Key were destroyed. Three hurricanes had come ashore on the same short stretch of coast in thirteen months.

P31: Remember the tide gauge at Cedar Key? We pulled its monthly highest water levels from NOAA and took the highest reading of each year, a hundred and one years of records going back to 1914. Here they are. For most of the century the yearly peak stays below four feet. A few storms in the 1990s and 2016 push it past five. Then Idalia, at nearly seven. And Helene, at nine point three. The two highest water levels in a hundred and one years came in the last two.

P32: NASA's PACE satellite photographed the Gulf the day after Helene came ashore. Along the Big Bend, the sea had turned pale and milky. The storm's winds and waves had churned sediment up from the seafloor across the shallow coastal water. For one day, the shelf that shaped this whole story was visible from space.

## Ending (calm)
P33: So here are the five reasons. A seafloor too shallow for a port or a beach. Ground made of soft, wet limestone full of holes. A boomtown that lost its trade to Tampa and its mills to a hurricane. Plans like the barge canal and the Foley mill that came and went. And a shelf that turns every big storm into a wall of water.

P34: And yet people stay. Cedar Key grew from six hundred and eighty-five people in 2020 to seven hundred and thirty-six in 2024, by the Census Bureau's estimate. Levy County grew by almost five thousand, Wakulla County, south of Tallahassee, by more than three thousand. Much of the coast is protected now, from the St. Marks refuge to the seagrass preserve offshore, and the same shallow water that kept the ports away feeds one of the biggest seagrass meadows in the Gulf.

P35: Maybe that's what the Big Bend really is: the part of Florida the sea never let anyone turn into the rest of Florida. So here's the question to leave you with. Should Florida keep the Big Bend mostly empty, or try once more to build on it? Tell me where you'd draw the line.
