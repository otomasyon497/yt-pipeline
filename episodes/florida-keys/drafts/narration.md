P0: The Florida Keys hang on a single road. It runs more than a hundred miles out into the sea, farther than New York is from Philadelphia.
[ON SCREEN: painted Florida map, the Keys red (cover view); the road draws itself south-west to Key West]

P2: So why would anyone build a highway across the sea, and can Florida keep it there?

## 1. A Town Worth the Trip (calm)

P5: Reason one. At the end of the chain sat a town that was, for a while, the biggest in Florida.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, Key West labelled, the Straits of Florida in blue]

P6: Key West has a deep, sheltered harbour on the Straits of Florida, the channel every ship takes between the Gulf of Mexico and the Atlantic. Cuba is about ninety miles away.
[ON SCREEN: map with the shipping lane arrow through the Straits; Havana labelled]

P7: The town grew rich on that position. Ships wrecked on the reef offshore, and Key West salvaged them, under a federal court that sat in the town to divide the cargo. Later it rolled cigars with tobacco from Cuba, and the Navy kept a base there.
[ON SCREEN: archive photos: Key West harbour, cigar factory (Florida Memory / LoC PD)]

P8: In the census of 1890, Key West had about eighteen thousand people. That was more than Jacksonville, and more than any other city in Florida.
[ON SCREEN: chart "People, 1890 census": Key West 18,080, Jacksonville 17,201 (R2)]

P8b: It was a town that kept losing its trades. Lighthouses made the reef safer, so fewer ships wrecked. Then most of the cigar makers moved to Tampa, on the mainland.
[ON SCREEN: photo of a reef lighthouse (PD); map arrow Key West to Tampa]

P9: But it had no road to the rest of the country. Everything came and went by ship.
[ON SCREEN: map, the chain of islands with open water gaps, no line]

P10: In 1905 the United States began to dig the Panama Canal. Key West was the closest deep-water American port to it.
[ON SCREEN: map zooms out: Key West, Cuba, Panama; arrow from Panama (R2)]

P11: A railroad owner named Henry Flagler saw a bet. His Florida East Coast Railway already ran down the coast to Miami and Homestead. If he could carry his tracks to Key West, the trade of the canal and of Cuba could come by rail.
[ON SCREEN: portrait of Flagler (PD); map with the FEC line down the east coast]

P12: The problem was the hundred and twenty-eight miles in between. Most of it was water.
[ON SCREEN: map, Homestead to Key West highlighted; label "128 MILES" (R1)]

P13: The road we drive today started as his railroad. And the railroad was a far harder thing to build.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the Long Key viaduct under construction]

## 2. A Railroad First (calm)

P14: Reason two. Before there was a road, there was a railroad across the sea.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, the 1912 line drawn island to island]

P15: Work began in 1905. Thousands of men cleared mangroves, dredged fill out of the shallows, and poured concrete into the water.
[ON SCREEN: construction photos (State Archives of Florida, PD)]

P16: The surveyors barely knew the islands. Halfway down Key Largo they found a lake no map had shown. The fill kept sinking into its soft bottom, and they named it Lake Surprise.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 Lake Surprise with US 1 crossing it; label]

P17: Then came the storms. In October 1906 a hurricane tore a floating barracks loose from its moorings, and about a hundred workers died. More hurricanes hit in 1909 and 1910.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "OCT 1906"; photo of a quarter boat (if PD found) or the works]

P18: Where the gaps were short, the crews built long rows of concrete arches. The Long Key Viaduct ran for about two miles just above the sea, and the railway put it on its posters.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the Long Key Viaduct (PD); Sentinel-2 view of the arches beside today's road]

P19: Where the water was deepest, at Bahia Honda, they built a steel truss bridge. West of Marathon came the longest gap of all, crossed by the Seven Mile Bridge. Its builders lived under it, on a five-acre island called Pigeon Key.
[ON SCREEN: map steps: Bahia Honda, Seven Mile Bridge, Pigeon Key labels; photo of Pigeon Key camp]

P19b: Even Key West itself was too small for the railway. There was no room on the island for a big rail yard and docks, so a dredging company pumped up the shallows and built about a hundred and thirty acres of new land, at Trumbo Point.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 Key West, Trumbo Point labelled]

P19c: The work never stopped for long. Men came and went by the thousand, because few stayed more than a season in the heat, the mosquitoes and the storms.
[ON SCREEN: photo of a work camp on the line (PD)]

P20: On the twenty-second of January 1912, the first train rolled into Key West. Flagler was on board. He was eighty-two years old, and he died the next year.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the first train arriving at Key West, 1912 (PD); stat card "22 JAN 1912"]

P20b: From 1915, ferries carried whole freight cars from Key West to Havana, so a wagon loaded in New York could be unloaded in Cuba.
[ON SCREEN: photo of a car ferry at Key West (PD); map arrow Key West to Havana]

P21: Then the business did not come. The canal opened in 1914, but the ships kept going where they had always gone. Freight on the single track stayed thin.
[ON SCREEN: map, arrow from Panama passes Key West by]

P22: Cars came too. In 1928 a road opened down the upper islands, but it stopped at Lower Matecumbe Key. From there, cars went on a ferry for about forty miles, a trip of around four hours, to the next stretch of road at No Name Key.
[ON SCREEN: map, road drawn, a 40-mile gap with a dashed ferry line]

P23: So for a few years, the Keys had two half-links to the mainland. Then the sea decided which one would stay.
[ON SCREEN: satellite of the open water at the ferry gap]

## 3. The Sea Took the Railroad (tension)

P24: Reason three. The road exists because the sea destroyed the railroad.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, the Upper and Middle Keys red]

P24b: By then Key West was in deep trouble. The Navy had closed its base, and in the Depression most of the town lived on relief. In July 1934 the city gave up and handed its powers to the state. Federal relief officials took over, and they decided Key West's future was tourists. The tourists would need a road.
[ON SCREEN: archive photo of Key West in the 1930s (PD, FERA/WPA); stat card "80 % ON RELIEF" (R15)]

P25: In 1934, the federal government sent hundreds of out-of-work veterans of the First World War to the Keys. They lived in camps of wooden huts and built bridges to close the ferry gap.
[ON SCREEN: photo of a veterans' camp (PD, FERA); map with the camps labelled at Windley Key and Lower Matecumbe]

P26: On Labor Day, the second of September 1935, a small hurricane crossed the Keys near Long Key. Its central pressure was 892 millibars. No storm on record has come ashore in the United States with lower pressure.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "892 mb"; map with the storm track]

P27: Its winds are now estimated at about a hundred and eighty-five miles an hour. The sea rose over the islands with it.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "185 MPH"]

P28: A train was sent from Miami to bring the veterans out. It was held at a drawbridge over the Miami River and reached Islamorada late, just as the surge did.
[ON SCREEN: map, the train route drawn south from Miami; Islamorada label]

P29: The water swept every car off the track. Only the locomotive stayed on the rails. Everyone on the train lived, but the camps were gone.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the overturned train cars at Islamorada, 1935 (PD)]

P30: The official count is 408 dead, and later counts went higher. More than half of them were veterans. About half of the men in the camps were away in Miami that weekend, or the toll would have been worse.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "408"; photo of the camp ruins]

P31: The storm washed away about forty miles of track. The railway was already bankrupt, and it did not rebuild.
[ON SCREEN: photo of washed-out railbed; map, the lost section red]

P32: In 1936 the state of Florida bought the whole right-of-way, with its bridges, for six hundred and forty thousand dollars.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "$640,000" (R4)]

P33: Then it laid a road on top. Concrete decks went over the old railway bridges. In 1938 the Overseas Highway opened, and for the first time you could drive to Key West.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the 1938 highway on the old viaduct; map, the road drawn the whole way]

P34: At Bahia Honda, the steel truss was too narrow for two lanes of cars. So the road was laid across the top of it, sixty-five feet above the water.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the Bahia Honda bridge with cars on the roof; Sentinel-2 of the old span ending in mid-air]

P34b: Two weeks after the storm, the writer Ernest Hemingway, who lived in Key West, published an article about the camps. Its title was a question: who murdered the vets? He blamed Washington for leaving the men on low islands in the hurricane season.
[ON SCREEN: photo of Hemingway in Key West, 1930s (PD); stat card "WHO MURDERED THE VETS?"]

P35: Today a stone memorial stands at Islamorada. The remains of some of the dead are inside it.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the 1935 Hurricane Monument (CC BY)]

P36: So the road won. But the road had a problem the railroad never solved. The islands it joined had nothing to drink.
[ON SCREEN: satellite of a Middle Keys island, no lakes]

## 4. Islands With No Water (curious)

P37: Reason four. The Keys cannot live without the mainland, because they have no fresh water of their own.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, the chain red, the mainland wellfield at Florida City labelled]

P38: The upper islands are the top of an old coral reef. The rock is full of holes. Rain sinks straight through it and mixes with the sea.
[ON SCREEN: photo of Key Largo limestone with fossil coral (Windley Key quarry, CC BY)]

P39: So for more than a century, Key West drank rain. Every house caught water from its roof in a cistern.
[ON SCREEN: photo of an old cistern; archive photo of Key West roofs]

P40: That worked for a town. It did not work for a naval base during a war.
[ON SCREEN: photo of Naval Station Key West, 1940s (PD Navy)]

P41: In 1941 the Navy bought land at Florida City on the mainland for wells. It laid a pipe down the old railroad route, island by island, about a hundred and thirty miles.
[ON SCREEN: map, the pipe drawn beside the road, blue]

P42: On the twenty-second of September 1942, fresh water from the mainland reached Key West for the first time. It took about six days to get there.
[ON SCREEN: archive photo of the pipeline being laid (KWAHS, check licence); stat card "22 SEP 1942"]

P43: That pipe changed what the Keys could be. With water you could build hotels, and fill pools, and live there all year. In 1940, about fourteen thousand people lived in the county. Twenty years later, nearly forty-eight thousand did.
[ON SCREEN: chart: Monroe County population by census, 1940-2020]

P44: Today the water still comes from the wellfield at Florida City. It runs down the chain in a big pipe beside the highway, across the same bridges, all the way to Key West.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 view of a bridge with the pipe line beside it; label "WATER MAIN"]

P44b: The pipe still carries about seventeen million gallons a day. The whole system now has more than a thousand miles of pipe, and it crosses forty-five bridges.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "17 MILLION GALLONS A DAY" (R7)]

P45: The power comes down the chain in the same way. So does the fuel, and almost all the food.
[ON SCREEN: map, three lines side by side: road, water, power]

P45b: And the people live along it too. We took every block of the 2020 census in the Keys and measured how far it lies from US 1.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, census blocks as dots along the road (M3)]

P45c: Half of all the people in the Keys live within a quarter of a mile of the highway. More than nine in ten live within a mile of it.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "93 % WITHIN 1 MILE"; push-in on a populated island with the road through it]

P45d: There is a backup. Two plants in the Keys can turn seawater into drinking water, and they are kept for the days when the pipe is cut. After Irma, they were switched on. The newer one, opened in 2025, makes about four million gallons a day, roughly a quarter of what the islands use.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the Stock Island reverse-osmosis plant (if licensed) or satellite of the site]

P46: Everything the islands need comes in through one narrow strip. And a strip that narrow can be closed.
[ON SCREEN: push-in on the mainland end of the road at Florida City]

## 5. One Road, One Door (tension)

P47: Reason five. A chain of islands with one road has one door. In 1982, somebody shut it.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, Florida City red, the road north of the Keys]

P48: Two years earlier, more than a hundred and twenty-five thousand Cubans had crossed the Straits in small boats. Most of them landed at Key West. On one day in May 1980, more than four and a half thousand arrived. The Border Patrol now watched the one road out.
[ON SCREEN: map, arrows from Mariel to Key West]

P49: In April 1982, the Border Patrol set up a roadblock on the highway just south of Florida City. Every car leaving the Keys was stopped and searched.
[ON SCREEN: map, a red bar across the road at Florida City; photo of US 1 at the mainland end]

P50: The queue ran for miles. Tourists stayed away. Key West's leaders went to a federal court, and the court refused to stop it.
[ON SCREEN: satellite of the road leaving the Keys]

P51: So on the twenty-third of April, the mayor of Key West read out a proclamation. The city had left the United States, he said, and was now the Conch Republic.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "23 APR 1982"; the Conch Republic flag (simple drawing)]

P52: It was a joke, and everyone knew it. But the roadblock came down soon after, and the Keys still celebrate the day.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the celebration (CC BY)]

P53: The joke was about a real thing. The same road that lets people in is the only way out. And in a hurricane, everyone has to use it at once.
[ON SCREEN: satellite of the Seven Mile Bridge, traffic]

P54: So Florida law sets a limit. All the permanent residents of the Keys must be able to drive out to the mainland in no more than twenty-four and a half hours.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "24.5 HOURS"; source Florida Statutes 380.0552 (R9)]

P54b: Until 2025, the limit was twenty-four hours. The state added half an hour, and ordered a new count of how many more homes that would allow.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "24 -> 24.5 HOURS, 2025" (R9)]

P55: That limit decides how many new homes may be built. More houses mean more cars on one road, so the state hands out building permits in small numbers each year.
[ON SCREEN: map, the road with dots of houses; stat card "PERMITS CAPPED"]

P56: The rule has a hard edge. People in mobile homes do not count as permanent residents, because they are ordered out first, before everyone else.
[ON SCREEN: satellite of a mobile home park on Big Pine Key]

P57: In September 2017, the door was tested. Hurricane Irma was heading for the Keys. On the morning of the sixth of September, all visitors were ordered to leave. That evening, the order went out to every resident.
[ON SCREEN: map, Irma's track; stat card "SEP 2017"]

P58: On the tenth of September it came ashore at Cudjoe Key, in the Lower Keys, as a category four storm.
[ON SCREEN: map, Cudjoe Key labelled]

P59: Nearly twelve hundred homes were destroyed. Most of them were mobile homes and small, older houses.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "1,179 HOMES DESTROYED" (Monroe County, R10); satellite before/after of a Big Pine Key park]

P60: Before anyone could go back, engineers had to inspect all forty-two bridges. Two short stretches of the highway had washed out near Marathon and had to be rebuilt first.
[ON SCREEN: map, the bridges dotted; washout near mile 74]

P61: The road came back within days. But the homes that blew away were the cheapest in the Keys, and with permits capped, many of them never came back.
[ON SCREEN: satellite of empty lots after Irma]

P62: The door has held, so far. But the threat to it now is not one storm. It is the sea itself.
[ON SCREEN: push-in on low road at water level]

## 6. The Sea Is Coming Up the Road (tension)

P63: Reason six. The road was built at the level of the sea in 1938. The sea is no longer there.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, the Keys with the tide gauge at Key West labelled]

P63b: How low is the land? We took fifteen hundred random points on the land of the Keys, mangrove islands included, and looked up the height of each one in the federal elevation survey.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, random dots over the islands (M2)]

P63c: Four-fifths of the land stands less than three feet above the sea. Nine-tenths is under five feet. The highest ground is only about twenty feet up.
[ON SCREEN: chart: share of the Keys' land below 3 ft / 5 ft / 10 ft (M2)]

P64: Key West has measured the sea since 1913, one of the longest tide records in the country. We took NOAA's monthly readings and averaged them.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the tide gauge / NOAA station]

P65: The sea at Key West is now about a foot higher than when the gauge began. And over the last thirty years it has risen more than twice as fast as the century before.
[ON SCREEN: chart: Key West yearly mean sea level 1913-2025 (M1)]

P66: On the islands that shows up as water in the streets, on days with no storm at all. In the autumn of 2019, the streets of one Key Largo neighbourhood, Stillwright Point, stayed under the tide for ninety-four days in a row.
[ON SCREEN: photo of a flooded Keys street (CC BY)]

P67: The county has worked out what it would take to raise its own roads, about a hundred and fifty miles of them, above the tides to come. The answer was about one point eight billion dollars.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "$1.8 BILLION" (R12)]

P68: The first neighbourhood is being rebuilt now, at Twin Lakes in Key Largo. Less than a mile of road, with pumps and drains, for about twenty-two million dollars.
[ON SCREEN: satellite of Twin Lakes; stat card "$22 MILLION / UNDER 1 MILE"]

P68b: The state has rebuilt the highway before. In the early 1980s it replaced thirty-seven of the forty-four old bridges, most of them Flagler's railway spans with a road on top. The new Seven Mile Bridge opened in 1982.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the new Seven Mile Bridge under construction, 1980s (Florida Memory, PD)]

P69: And that is only the side streets. The highway belongs to the state. Its longest bridge, the Seven Mile Bridge of 1982, is likely to be replaced, and the state's early estimate for a new one is about six hundred and seventy-seven million dollars.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 Seven Mile Bridge with the old bridge beside it; stat card "$677 MILLION"]

P69b: Even the reason most visitors come is changing. In the summer of 2023 the sea around the Keys grew hotter than it had been in any record, and nearly all the remaining elkhorn and staghorn coral on the reef died. Scientists now call both species functionally extinct there.
[ON SCREEN: photo of bleached elkhorn coral (NOAA, PD)]

P70: About eighty thousand people live in the Keys. Every road raised, every bridge replaced, is paid for by them, and by everyone else in Florida.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "~80,000 PEOPLE" (Census 2025 est.)]

P71: The old Seven Mile Bridge is still there, beside the new one. It is a footpath now, and a piece of it is missing in the middle, where boats pass through.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the old Seven Mile Bridge with its gap (CC BY)]

## Ending (calm)

P72: So this is why the Florida Keys hang on a single road.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, the full road, red chain (cover view again)]

P73: A town at the end of the chain was worth reaching. A railroad reached it, and the storm of 1935 took the railroad and left the road. The islands had no water, so the water came down the road too. And because it is the only road, it is also the only way out, the reason the Keys are allowed to grow so little.
[ON SCREEN: the six reasons as map steps along the road, quick]

P74: The roadblock of 1982 showed what one door means. The storm of 1935 showed what the sea can take. The price of keeping the road is the open question.
[ON SCREEN: photo 1935 train; Conch Republic flag; stat card "$1.8 BILLION"]

P74b: The numbers we measured say how tight the bet is. Nine in ten people live within a mile of one road. Four-fifths of the land stands less than three feet above the sea. And that sea is about a foot higher than it was when the gauge at Key West began.
