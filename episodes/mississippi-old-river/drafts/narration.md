# Why the Mississippi Wants to Leave New Orleans — narration draft (sourced: research.md, OK rows only)

## Hook (curious)
P0: Every second, about six hundred thousand cubic feet of water slide past New Orleans. This is the Mississippi near the end of its journey, carrying rain from all or part of thirty-one states, about forty percent of the continental United States. But upstream, about fifty miles northwest of Baton Rouge, this river is trying to leave. Why does the biggest river in the country want to abandon its own cities, and what is holding it back?

P1: The answer involves a fire set on a log jam twenty miles long, a government geologist who gave the river a deadline, and a spring in 1973 when the floodwater dug a hole under the gates that hold it in place. It also involves one of the few stretches of the Louisiana coast that are still growing, and a summer when the Gulf of Mexico crept up the riverbed toward the drinking water of New Orleans.

P2: Along the river's last stretch sit the busiest port in the United States by tonnage, a long row of refineries and chemical plants, and the water intakes of the city itself. All of it depends on the Mississippi staying exactly where it is. The first reason it might not is that this river has never stayed in one place for long.

## 1. Rivers Like This Don't Stay Put (calm)
P3: A river that builds a delta is always building its own trap. When the water reaches the sea it slows down and drops its mud, and over centuries the mouth creeps further out into the Gulf. Every mile of new land makes the route to the sea longer and the slope gentler. The water slows even more, drops more sediment in its own bed, and the bed rises. In time, the river is running higher than the land around it, held in by the banks it built itself.

P4: Then one big flood breaks through a bank, finds a shorter and steeper way down to the sea, and the river takes it. Geologists call that jump an avulsion. The old channel is left behind with only a trickle of water, and the river starts building a brand new delta somewhere else.

P5: The lower Mississippi has done this again and again. According to the Army Corps of Engineers, in the five to six thousand years since the sea rose close to today's level, the river below Baton Rouge has migrated back and forth across Louisiana at least seven times, each time building a new delta. Geologists who dated the buried deposits counted sixteen separate delta lobes. Left alone, NASA's Earth Observatory notes, the lower river would probably swing across a two-hundred-mile arc every few thousand years.

P6: In 1944, the geologist Harold Fisk published maps of the river's ancient courses for the Mississippi River Commission, every abandoned channel in its own colour. They look like tangled ribbons, because that is what the valley floor really is: thousands of years of a river that kept changing its mind.

P7: You can still see the old routes from space. This thin line of towns south of Donaldsonville follows Bayou Lafourche, which carried the main flow of the Mississippi within the last couple of thousand years. The houses stand on the strip of high ground the old river built along its banks, with long, narrow fields running back from the road into the marsh. Further west, Bayou Teche traces another course the river abandoned thousands of years ago.

P8: Every one of those moves started the same way: a shorter road opened up, and the river found it. Today, a shorter road runs right beside the Mississippi, and for most of the last two centuries, people have been widening it.

## 2. A Shorter Road Next Door (tension)
P9: About fifty miles northwest of Baton Rouge, three rivers almost touch. The Mississippi comes down from the north, the Red River comes in from the west, and the Atchafalaya leaves toward the south. According to the Corps, sometime around the fifteenth century a westward loop of the Mississippi, later called Turnbull's Bend, cut into the valley of the Red River and captured it.

P10: Back then, the Atchafalaya was only a small branch flowing out of the Red, a few miles to the south. Once the loop had joined the two systems, the Red stopped flowing into the Mississippi and flowed into the Atchafalaya instead, and a channel connected all three. Usually the current ran west, out of the Mississippi and into the Atchafalaya.

P11: Now look at where each road goes. From here the Mississippi winds south and east, past Baton Rouge and New Orleans and out through its delta: three hundred and fifteen miles to the Gulf of Mexico. The Atchafalaya runs almost straight south, through the largest river swamp in the country, to the Gulf at Atchafalaya Bay: one hundred and forty-two miles. Less than half the distance, for the same drop to sea level.

P12: Water does not read maps, but it always follows the slope. A steeper channel runs faster, a faster current digs its bed deeper and wider, and a bigger channel draws in still more water, which digs it deeper again. For a long time the Atchafalaya could not start that cycle. Two things stood in the way, and people removed both of them.

## 3. People Opened the Door (tension)
P13: The first was the loop itself. In 1831, Captain Henry Miller Shreve, a famous steamboat man and the founder of Shreveport, dug a cut across the narrow neck of Turnbull's Bend. The Mississippi accepted the shortcut and abandoned its old channel. The upper part of the loop silted up over the years, but the lower part stayed open, a short channel tying the Mississippi to the Red and the Atchafalaya. People called it, simply, Old River.

P14: The second obstacle was a raft. Not a boat: a log jam, built up from drifting trees over decades, that blocked the head of the Atchafalaya for some twenty to thirty miles. It may have started forming in the late seventeen hundreds, and settlers had failed to break it apart. As long as it sat there, the Atchafalaya stayed a minor stream.

P15: In 1839, during a drought, the water was so low that people crossed the channel on a plank fifteen feet long. Local residents set the raft on fire. According to an account quoted in a 1952 report for the Mississippi River Commission, the fire burned about twenty miles of it down to the waterline. The state of Louisiana sent snagboats to pull out what was left, and by 1855 the last of it was gone.

P16: This is how such a jam looked, on the Red River upstream, being cleared in 1873. Imagine twenty miles of it, and then imagine it gone. Without the raft, the Atchafalaya was a free, straight, steep channel to the sea, connected to the biggest river in the country.

P17: The door was open. An 1881 report of the Mississippi River Commission noted that rapid enlargement of the Atchafalaya had begun. In 1900 it was taking about five percent of the Mississippi's flow. By 1950 it was taking about a quarter. Counting the Red River too, its share had doubled from fifteen percent to thirty in fifty years. Every year the shortcut got bigger, and every year it pulled a little harder.

## 4. The Deadline and the Gates (tension)
P18: In 1952, Harold Fisk, the same geologist who had mapped the river's old courses, delivered a report on the Atchafalaya to the Mississippi River Commission. He measured the trend and followed it forward. Sometime between 1965 and 1975, he wrote, the Atchafalaya would reach a critical stage, taking about forty percent of the flow. After that, closure of the Mississippi below Old River would be rapid, and the diversion uncontrollable.

P19: According to NASA's Earth Observatory, the Corps concluded in 1953 that, left unchecked, the Mississippi would change course by about 1990. The Corps' own description of that future is blunt. The old channel past Baton Rouge and New Orleans would become a saltwater estuary. The refineries, chemical plants, grain elevators and power plants along it all depend on that fresh water. Cities down to New Orleans would be hard-pressed to find drinking water. And the Atchafalaya Basin could not take the whole Mississippi without massive flooding.

P20: So on the third of September 1954, Congress authorized a project to hold the river where it was. The rule was simple: freeze the river the way it was in 1950. About seventy percent of the combined flow of the Mississippi and the Red stays in the Mississippi, and about thirty percent goes down the Atchafalaya, averaged over the year.

P21: Construction began in 1955. The Corps built the first structures in a dry hole beside the river and only then dredged the channels that connected them. The Low Sill Structure has eleven gates, each forty-four feet wide. Next to it, the Overbank Structure, seventy-three bays long, takes extra water in flood years. Old River itself was dammed, and a navigation lock opened in 1963 so boats could still pass between the rivers.

P22: The structures went into operation in 1962. By then the complex had cost sixty-seven million dollars. For the first time, the shortcut had a gate on it. The Corps could decide how much of the Mississippi the Atchafalaya was allowed to have, and for about ten years, the river let them. Then came the spring of 1973.

## 5. April 1973 (tension)
P23: The autumn of 1972 had been wet across the Mississippi valley, and the spring of 1973 was worse. The river crested again and again, and a long flood fight raged up and down the valley. The federal photographers sent to document it recorded that the Mississippi had reached its highest level in more than one hundred and fifty years. Across Louisiana, homes and camps went under water.

P24: At Old River, the Low Sill Structure took the brunt. By the Corps' account, the Mississippi below the structure could not carry as much water as it had in 1950, while the Atchafalaya had been deepening and flowing faster, demanding even more. The gates did regulate the flow. But the turbulence of the water scoured the ground around them.

P25: In April 1973, the floodwater undermined a concrete wing wall sixty-seven feet high on the south end of the structure, the wall that guided the river into the gates, and it collapsed. Under the structure, it was worse. A large scour hole had opened in front of and beneath the Low Sill. The structure stood on steel pilings ninety feet long, and about fifty feet of them were now exposed.

P26: The gate that was supposed to hold the Mississippi in place was standing on bare stilts over a hole, with the river at its highest in a century and a half. Downstream, the Corps opened the Morganza Floodway for the first time since its completion in 1954. Its one hundred and twenty-five gates can send up to six hundred thousand cubic feet a second into the Atchafalaya Basin, away from Baton Rouge and New Orleans.

P27: The Low Sill held. When the water went down, the Corps built a stone dike to replace the wall and pumped grout into the void beneath the structure; the rehabilitation took until 1981. But the Low Sill would never again be trusted at full strength. It had been designed for a difference in water level of thirty-seven feet across its gates. After 1973, the Corps limited it to no more than twenty-two.

P28: So the Corps built a second line of defence. The Auxiliary Structure was started in July 1981 and finished in December 1986, for two hundred and six million dollars: six gates, each sixty-two feet wide. Here is the site in 1985, still a construction ground, and here it is today. The new channel is the Auxiliary Structure, and beside the Low Sill now stands a power plant.

P29: That is the Sidney A. Murray Junior hydroelectric station. It was built far downriver, near New Orleans, then towed two hundred and eight miles up the Mississippi and went online in 1990. It turns the water the Atchafalaya is allowed to take into up to one hundred and ninety-two megawatts of electricity. Together, the structures here can pass about seven hundred thousand cubic feet of water a second.

P30: Since then, the system has been tested again. In May 2011, during another enormous flood, the Corps opened Morganza for only the second time: two bays on the first day, seventeen by late May. In 2019 Morganza was readied and then left shut, while the Bonnet Carré Spillway above New Orleans had to be opened twice in the same year for the first time. Every big flood is one more test of the same concrete.

## 6. The River Is Already Winning (curious)
P31: Old River Control has held for more than sixty years. The Atchafalaya still gets its thirty percent and no more. But thirty percent of the Mississippi is a great deal of water, and it brings mud with it. Follow it south to the sea, and you find something this coast has very little of: new land.

P32: In 1941 the Corps dug a second mouth for the Atchafalaya, the Wax Lake Outlet, to lower flood levels at Morgan City. Today it carries about forty percent of the Atchafalaya's water. Mud began building up in the bay in the 1950s, and after the great flood of 1973, new land rose above the water for the first time.

P33: Here is that stretch of coast in 1986: only a few mud bars. And here it is in 2025, with two young deltas, branching like trees. We measured them on Landsat images: between 1985 and 2026, the Wax Lake delta gained about fifty-five square kilometers of land and the Atchafalaya delta about sixty. That is roughly two point eight square kilometers a year, the same rate scientists at Louisiana State University have estimated.

P34: Almost everywhere else, the coast is going the other way. The US Geological Survey found that coastal Louisiana lost about four thousand eight hundred square kilometers of land between 1932 and 2016, about a quarter of what it had. At the worst of it, in the 1970s, a football field of land disappeared every thirty-four minutes. And the river now brings far less mud to rebuild it: about one hundred and seventy million tonnes a year, NASA estimates, down from about four hundred.

P35: Where the river is allowed to spill part of its load, the land grows. It is a small picture of what the Mississippi did for thousands of years, and of what it is still trying to do.

## What Leaving Would Look Like (calm)
P36: The other half of that picture has already had a preview. The bed of the Mississippi between Natchez and the Gulf lies below sea level. When the river runs low, there is too little current to push back, and the heavier salt water of the Gulf creeps upstream along the bottom, like a wedge under the fresh water.

P37: The Corps has built underwater barriers across the riverbed near Myrtle Grove to stop it, in 1988, 1999, 2012, 2022 and again in 2023. That year the river fell so low that on the twentieth of September the salt water flowed over the top of the sill. The Corps raised its crest from fifty-five feet below to thirty, and barges carried fresh water down to Plaquemines Parish, where the salt had reached the water intakes.

P38: That was a few months of low water. If the Mississippi ever did take the Atchafalaya, the old channel would be low all year. In the Corps' words, it would become a saltwater estuary, right through the stretch where the Port of South Louisiana alone handled more than two hundred and seventeen million tons of cargo in 2023, and where New Orleans takes its water from the river.

P39: For now, the gates hold. Every year the Corps gives the Atchafalaya its thirty percent and keeps the rest in the old channel. But the shortcut is still there: one hundred and forty-two miles to the sea, against three hundred and fifteen. Should the Corps keep holding the Mississippi in place forever, or let more of it go and rebuild the coast?
