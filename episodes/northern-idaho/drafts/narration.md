## Hook (curious)

P0: Northern Idaho is a quarter of the state, a block of mountains and forest bigger than Vermont and New Hampshire put together.
[ON SCREEN: painted map of the US Northwest, Idaho amber, the ten northern counties red; Vermont and New Hampshire outlines laid over it]

P1: But only about one Idahoan in five lives there, and most of them crowd into one corner. Idaho County, the biggest in the state, covers more than eight thousand square miles. Only sixteen thousand people live in it.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "1 IN 5" (M1); map, Idaho County highlighted; stat card "2 PEOPLE PER SQ MILE"]

P2: So why does almost nobody live in northern Idaho?
[ON SCREEN: question card over the red north]

P3: Even the map looks strange. A thin chimney of land climbs north between Washington and Montana, until at the Canadian border it is only about forty-five miles wide. And above the Salmon River, Idaho runs on a different clock: the north keeps Pacific time with Seattle, the south keeps Mountain time with Boise.
[ON SCREEN: map, the panhandle draws itself, label "45 MILES"; the Salmon River drawn blue across the state; north red "PACIFIC", south alt "MOUNTAIN"]

P4: The answer runs through a capital that was carried off by soldiers, a fire that burned an area nearly as big as Connecticut in two days, and a valley that dug up more than a billion ounces of silver and then poisoned its own children.
[ON SCREEN: Lewiston's first capitol photo; 1910 fire photo; Silver Valley from orbit]

P5: There are six reasons, and the first one is a wall of mountains in the middle of the state.
[ON SCREEN: shaded relief of Idaho, mountains rising between north and south]

## 1. The Wall in the Middle (calm)

P6: Reason one. Northern Idaho is cut off from the rest of its own state.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, north red, south alt; a mountain band drawn between them]

P7: Between Coeur d'Alene in the north and Boise in the south lie the Clearwater, the Salmon River and the Bitterroot mountains. It is some of the roughest country in the lower forty-eight states.
[ON SCREEN: map steps: labels "Clearwater Mountains", "Salmon River Mountains", "Bitterroot Range"; Sentinel-2 of the Salmon River canyon]

P8: Much of it was never built on at all. Along the Salmon River lies the Frank Church River of No Return Wilderness, almost two and a half million acres with no roads. It is the largest single block of wilderness in the lower forty-eight. Next to it lie the Selway-Bitterroot and the Gospel Hump wildernesses, and around them millions more acres of roadless forest.
[ON SCREEN: map, the wildernesses in green; stat card "2.37 MILLION ACRES"; photo of the Salmon River]

P8b: The river through it is called the River of No Return. In the 1800s, freight boats could ride its rapids downstream but never come back up, so at the end of each trip the boatmen broke them up and sold the wood.
[ON SCREEN: photo of a Salmon River scow or rapids; label "RIVER OF NO RETURN"]

P8c: On the western edge, the Snake River runs through Hells Canyon, the deepest river gorge in North America, almost eight thousand feet from rim to river.
[ON SCREEN: map, Hells Canyon labelled on the Oregon line; photo of Hells Canyon; stat card "7,993 FT"]

P9: So there is one road between north and south. US Highway 95 is mostly two lanes, and for about forty years it climbed over a single grade at White Bird Hill, a road of switchbacks that was the only link between the two halves of the state. The faster road over the hill opened only in 1975.
[ON SCREEN: map, US 95 drawn north to south; photo of the old White Bird grade; stat card "1975"]

P10: East to west it was no easier. The highway along the Lochsa River over Lolo Pass, close to the route Lewis and Clark took in 1805, was finished only in 1962.
[ON SCREEN: map, US 12 drawn east over Lolo Pass; photo of the Lochsa River; stat card "1962"]

P11: We asked a route planner for the drive from Sandpoint in the panhandle to Boise. The road through Idaho is about four hundred and twenty miles. The road that leaves Idaho, through Washington and Oregon, is seventy miles longer, and it takes about the same time.
[ON SCREEN: map, two routes drawn: red through Idaho, blue through Washington and Oregon; stat card "423 MI vs 496 MI" (M3)]

P12: That is why the north sets its clocks by Seattle. Spokane, just across the Washington line, is its big city. It shops there, flies from there and watches its television.
[ON SCREEN: map, Spokane and Coeur d'Alene 30 miles apart; Sentinel-2 of the I-90 corridor between them]

P13: And when people could not get through the mountains, they could not farm them either, which brings us to the second reason: who owns the land.
[ON SCREEN: photo of forested ridges]

## 2. The Land Is Not for Sale (calm)

P14: Reason two. Most of Idaho does not belong to anyone who could live on it.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, federal land shaded over Idaho]

P15: About sixty-two per cent of the state is federal land, mostly national forest. Only Nevada and Utah have a larger share. In the north the forests run almost unbroken from the Canadian border to the Salmon River. Its two national forests, the Idaho Panhandle and the Nez Perce-Clearwater, cover about seven million acres between them.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "62 % FEDERAL"; map steps: national forests labelled (Idaho Panhandle, Nez Perce-Clearwater)]

P16: Those forests were set aside for timber and water, not for towns. And in the summer of 1910 they showed what else they could do.
[ON SCREEN: photo of dense cedar forest]

P17: It had been a dry year. By August hundreds of small fires were burning across northern Idaho and western Montana. Then, on the twentieth of August, a hurricane-force wind came from the southwest.
[ON SCREEN: map, fire area drawn red over the Idaho-Montana line; stat card "20 AUGUST 1910"]

P18: In two days the fires joined into one. They burned about three million acres, nearly as big as Connecticut. About eighty-five people died, most of them firefighters. A third of the mining town of Wallace burned.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "3 MILLION ACRES"; photo of Wallace after the fire; stat card "~85 DEAD"]

P18b: That night the mayor of Wallace ordered the women and children out. Trains carried them west to Spokane and east to Missoula, and soldiers pulled men off the cars to keep them on the fire line.
[ON SCREEN: photo of Wallace 1910; map, rail lines to Spokane and Missoula]

P19: A forest ranger called Ed Pulaski was caught in the fire with a crew of about forty-five men. He led them into an old mine tunnel, held them there at gunpoint when they panicked, and hung wet blankets across the entrance. All but five or six of them survived.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the Pulaski tunnel; portrait of Ed Pulaski]

P20: The Big Burn made the young Forest Service what it became. From then on its job was to stop every fire, and by 1935 the rule was to have each one out by ten o'clock the next morning. The tool Pulaski designed after the fire, half axe and half hoe, is still carried by wildland firefighters today.
[ON SCREEN: photo of a Pulaski tool; stat card "10 AM RULE"]

P21: The land stayed with the government, and the government kept it as forest. But to understand why the north feels it has never had a say, we have to go back to the people who drew its borders.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, Idaho Territory 1863]

## 3. A Territory Run from Far Away (curious)

P22: Reason three. Northern Idaho was settled first, and then it lost the state.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, Idaho Territory in 1863 covering Montana and Wyoming too]

P23: In 1860 a prospector called Elias Pierce found gold on the Clearwater River. Within a year thousands of miners had arrived, and a tent town grew where the Clearwater meets the Snake. They called it Lewiston.
[ON SCREEN: map, Pierce and Lewiston pinned; Sentinel-2 of the confluence at Lewiston]

P24: The gold was on Nez Perce land. In 1863 the United States made the Nez Perce sign a new treaty that cut their reservation by about ninety per cent. Many bands never signed it, and the anger it left led to the Nez Perce War of 1877.
[ON SCREEN: map, the 1855 reservation and the 1863 reservation; stat card "-90 %"]

P24b: In June 1877, at White Bird Canyon, below the hill that US 95 climbs today, Nez Perce warriors routed a column of US cavalry. It was the first battle of the war. The bands that refused the treaty then fled east over the Lolo Trail, about eleven hundred and seventy miles through four states in a hundred and twenty-six days. They were caught in Montana in October, about forty miles short of Canada, and Chief Joseph surrendered.
[ON SCREEN: map, White Bird Canyon pinned; the flight route drawn east to the Bear Paw; portrait of Chief Joseph; stat card "1,170 MILES"]

P25: The same year, Congress created Idaho Territory, and Lewiston became its first capital. But the miners had already moved on, south to the Boise Basin, where richer gold had been found. By 1864 most of the territory's voters lived in the south.
[ON SCREEN: photo of Lewiston's first capitol building; map, an arrow from Lewiston south to the Boise Basin]

P26: In December 1864 the legislature voted to move the capital to Boise. Lewiston refused. So in 1865 the acting governor came back with soldiers from Fort Lapwai and took the territorial seal and the archives south. The territory's supreme court settled it for Boise in 1866.
[ON SCREEN: map, the route Lewiston - Boise; stat card "1865"; photo of the Idaho territorial seal]

P27: Then the shape. In 1864 Congress cut Montana Territory out of Idaho. Montana's border was drawn along the crest of the Bitterroot Mountains, and in the north Idaho was left with only a narrow strip of land.
[ON SCREEN: painted map 1864, Montana cut out, the Bitterroot line drawn; the panhandle left red]

P28: The north never liked being run from Boise, nearly three hundred miles away over the mountains. In 1887 Congress actually passed a bill to give the panhandle to Washington Territory. Idaho's governor sent a telegram to President Grover Cleveland, and Cleveland let the bill die without signing it.
[ON SCREEN: map, the panhandle sliding into Washington; stat card "1887"; portrait of Grover Cleveland]

P28b: The territory still had to win the north back. In January 1889 its governor signed the bill that founded the University of Idaho, and the university was put in Moscow, in the north. It helped stop the push to join Washington, and it is still there.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the University of Idaho administration building; map, Moscow pinned; stat card "1889"]

P29: Three years later, in 1890, Idaho became a state with the panhandle still attached. Should the north have gone to Washington? People in North Idaho still argue about it. What they did have was something the south did not: silver.
[ON SCREEN: map, Idaho 1890; Sentinel-2 of the Silver Valley]

## 4. Silver, Dynamite and Lead (tension)

P30: Reason four. The one place in the north that drew thousands of people also did the most damage.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, the Silver Valley red along I-90 east of Coeur d'Alene]

P31: In the 1880s prospectors found silver, lead and zinc along the South Fork of the Coeur d'Alene River. The narrow canyon there became the Silver Valley, with Wallace, Kellogg and Mullan strung along the river.
[ON SCREEN: map steps: Wallace, Kellogg, Mullan pinned; photo of Wallace; Sentinel-2 of Kellogg]

P31b: Wallace is so tight in its canyon that Interstate 90 once ran down its main street. Until 1991 it had the last traffic light on the whole interstate between Seattle and Boston. When the freeway was finally lifted over the town on a viaduct, the people of Wallace held a funeral for the light.
[ON SCREEN: photo of downtown Wallace; Sentinel-2 of Wallace and the I-90 viaduct; stat card "1991"]

P32: Since 1884 the district has produced more than a billion ounces of silver. It is one of the richest silver districts ever found on Earth.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "1 BILLION+ OZ"; photo of a Silver Valley mine headframe]

P33: The money did not reach the men underground. In 1892 and again in 1899 the miners' union fought the mine owners. On the twenty-ninth of April 1899, union men seized a train, loaded it with about three thousand pounds of dynamite, and blew up the Bunker Hill company's mill at Wardner.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the destroyed Bunker Hill concentrator; stat card "29 APRIL 1899"]

P34: Governor Frank Steunenberg declared martial law. Federal troops came, and hundreds of miners were held for months in open-air pens called the bullpen.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the bullpen; map, Wardner pinned]

P35: Six years later, on the thirtieth of December 1905, Steunenberg opened the gate of his house in Caldwell and a bomb killed him. The man who planted it, Harry Orchard, said union leaders had paid him. The union's leader Big Bill Haywood was tried for the murder in 1907, in one of the most famous trials in American labour history, and acquitted.
[ON SCREEN: portrait of Frank Steunenberg; stat card "30 DEC 1905"]

P36: The mines kept going. On the second of May 1972, a fire broke out deep inside the Sunshine Mine near Kellogg. One hundred and seventy-three men were underground. Smoke and carbon monoxide filled the tunnels, and ninety-one of them died. The cause was never found. It led to the federal mine safety law of 1977.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the Sunshine Mine memorial; stat card "91 MINERS"; stat card "1977 MINE ACT"]

P37: The worst damage came from above the ground. In Kellogg the Bunker Hill smelter melted lead ore. In September 1973 a fire damaged the baghouse, the great filter that caught the smoke. The company kept the smelter running without it.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the Bunker Hill smelter stacks; stat card "SEPT 1973"]

P38: Lead dust settled over the town. When the children of Kellogg were tested, their blood lead levels were some of the highest ever measured in the United States.
[ON SCREEN: map, a circle around Kellogg; stat card "HIGHEST EVER MEASURED"]

P39: The smelter closed in 1981. Two years later the valley became one of the country's first Superfund sites. Crews have since replaced the soil or laid clean barriers over more than seven thousand homes and businesses.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 of the Bunker Hill site today; stat card "7,000+ PROPERTIES"; stat card "SUPERFUND 1983"]

P39b: And the waste did not stay in the valley. Until 1968 most of the mines' tailings went straight into the river. About seventy-five million metric tons of sediment rich in lead and other metals now lie on the bed of Lake Coeur d'Alene, downstream.
[ON SCREEN: map, the South Fork and the river drawn into Lake Coeur d'Alene; Sentinel-2 of the river's delta; stat card "75 MILLION TONS"]

P40: In 1970 Kellogg had about thirty-eight hundred people. In 2020 it had about twenty-three hundred. And the Silver Valley was not the only industry up here that collapsed.
[ON SCREEN: photo of Kellogg today]

P40b: Kellogg has tried to turn itself around. In 1990 it opened a ski area, Silver Mountain, with a gondola that climbs three point one miles from the edge of town, the longest single-stage gondola in North America.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the Silver Mountain gondola; stat card "3.1 MILES"]

## 5. When the Mills Closed (calm)

P41: Reason five. The forests that covered the north paid its wages for a century, and then they stopped.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, timber towns pinned: Potlatch, Orofino, Pierce, St. Maries]

P42: Logging came with the railways. In 1906 the Potlatch Lumber Company built a whole town of its own on the edge of the Palouse, and next to it the largest white pine sawmill in the world.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the Potlatch mill; map, Potlatch pinned]

P43: Towns like Orofino, Pierce and St. Maries lived on the logs that came out of the national forests. Then, from the 1980s, there were fewer of them. Less federal land was opened for logging, machines replaced workers, and cheaper lumber came from Canada.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 of clearcuts near Pierce; stat card "1980s"]

P44: The great mill at Potlatch closed in 1983. The plywood plant near Pierce closed in 2000, and 215 people lost their jobs. In 2016 two sawmills near Orofino closed within six months of each other.
[ON SCREEN: stat cards "1983", "2000: 215 JOBS"; photo of Orofino]

P45: Where the mills closed, the young left, and the towns grew older. Should the national forests be opened to more logging to bring the jobs back? Or are they worth more standing, for water, wildlife and the people who come to see them? Idaho has argued about it for forty years.
[ON SCREEN: photo of a forest road; question card]

P46: So we have mountains, government land, a lost capital, a poisoned valley and closed mills. And yet in one corner of the north, people are arriving faster than almost anywhere in the country.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, Kootenai County red]

## 6. Where People Do Live (curious)

P47: Reason six is really the answer to the whole question. Northern Idaho is not empty. It is full in one place and empty everywhere else.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, the north red, Kootenai County bright]

P48: We added up the 2020 census for the ten northern counties. Kootenai County, around Coeur d'Alene, holds almost half of the north's people on six per cent of its land. Two in three northern Idahoans live within about fifteen miles of just three towns: Coeur d'Alene, Lewiston and Moscow.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "47 % ON 6 % OF THE LAND" (M1); map, circles around the three towns; stat card "2 IN 3"]

P48b: Lewiston, at the southern end, even has a seaport. Barges come up the Columbia and the Snake through eight locks and dams, about four hundred and sixty-five river miles from the Pacific, and carry wheat, peas and lentils from the Palouse back down. It is the most inland seaport on the West Coast.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 of the Port of Lewiston; map, the barge route to the Pacific; stat card "465 RIVER MILES"]

P48c: For decades the corner around Coeur d'Alene also had a problem with its name. From the 1970s a white supremacist group, the Aryan Nations, had its compound near Hayden Lake. In 2000 a local jury ordered it to pay six point three million dollars to a mother and son its guards had fired on. The group went bankrupt, and the compound was torn down in 2001.
[ON SCREEN: photo of Hayden Lake; stat card "$6.3 MILLION, 2000"]

P49: And Kootenai is growing. Between 2020 and 2024 it gained about seventeen thousand people, almost ten per cent. Most of the newcomers came from other states, many of them from Spokane and from Southern California.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "+9.9 %"; Sentinel-2 of new housing in Post Falls]

P50: It works because Coeur d'Alene is not really cut off. It sits on Interstate 90, half an hour from Spokane and its airport. The road follows the line of the Mullan Road, the first wagon road over these mountains, built between 1859 and 1862.
[ON SCREEN: map, I-90 drawn from Spokane through Coeur d'Alene over Fourth of July Pass; stat card "1862"]

P51: North of Coeur d'Alene lies Lake Pend Oreille, Idaho's largest lake. It is about eleven hundred and fifty feet deep, carved by the ice age.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 of Lake Pend Oreille; stat card "1,150 FT"]

P52: In 1942 the US Navy built a training base on its shore, hundreds of miles from any ocean, where it was thought to be safe from Japanese bombers. In about two and a half years almost three hundred thousand sailors trained there. For a while it was the second-largest naval training station in the country.
[ON SCREEN: photo of Farragut Naval Training Station; stat card "293,381 SAILORS"]

P53: The base closed after the war and became Farragut State Park. But the Navy never left the lake. Its deep, quiet water is still used to test large scale models of submarines and ships.
[ON SCREEN: photo of a Navy submarine model on the lake; map, Bayview pinned]

P54: Now drive south from Coeur d'Alene into Idaho County, and it is a different place. Sixteen thousand people. More than eight thousand square miles, more land than New Jersey. Grangeville, its largest town, has about three thousand people.
[ON SCREEN: map, Idaho County highlighted; stat card "8,477 SQ MI"; photo of Grangeville]

## Ending (calm)

P55: So why does almost nobody live in northern Idaho?
[ON SCREEN: painted map, the north red, as in the hook]

P56: Because a wall of mountains cuts it off from the rest of its state, with one road over White Bird Hill and the last highway finished in 1962. Because most of its land is federal forest, and in 1910 that forest burned on a scale nobody had seen.
[ON SCREEN: quick recap: White Bird photo; 1910 fire photo]

P57: Because its capital was carried off to Boise by soldiers in 1865, and the bill that would have given it to Washington died on a president's desk. Because the Silver Valley gave a billion ounces of silver and left lead in its children's blood. And because the mills that replaced the mines closed one after another.
[ON SCREEN: territorial seal photo; Bunker Hill photo; Potlatch mill photo]

P58: What is left is a north that is full in one corner around Coeur d'Alene and nearly empty everywhere else, where the clocks still run on Pacific time.
[ON SCREEN: map, the Kootenai corner bright, the rest dark red; label "PACIFIC TIME"]

P59: If you lived in North Idaho, would you rather be governed from Boise, or from Olympia in Washington?
[ON SCREEN: question card; the panhandle on the map between Boise and Olympia]
