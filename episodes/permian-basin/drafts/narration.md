# Why Half of America's Oil Comes From One Empty Corner of Texas — narration draft (sourced: research.md)

## Hook (curious)
P0: From orbit, this part of West Texas looks almost empty. Brown scrub, dry riverbeds, a few straight roads. But zoom in, and the desert is covered in pale squares, thousands of them, laid out in rows like a grid on graph paper. Each square is a drilling pad. And together, these squares and the ones across the border in New Mexico produced about six point six million barrels of crude oil a day in 2025. According to the U.S. Energy Information Administration, that is forty-eight percent of all the crude oil produced in the United States.

P1: Nearly half of the oil of the world's biggest oil producer comes out of one dry basin, about two hundred and fifty miles wide and three hundred long. The biggest towns here, Midland and Odessa, have about a hundred and forty-four thousand and a hundred and twenty thousand people. There is no great city, no big river, no coast. So why here?

P2: The answer is that this desert used to be a sea. Over the next few minutes you'll see a reef that became the highest mountain in Texas, caves dissolved by acid that rose out of the oil, a layer of salt so thick that the United States buries nuclear waste in it, and a well on university land that changed the state's history. And you'll see what it costs to pump a dead sea back out of the ground, from earthquakes to holes that open in the desert. It starts about two hundred and sixty million years ago, when this corner of Texas was underwater.

## 1. A Sea at the Edge of Pangea (calm)
P3: In the Permian period, all of the world's continents were jammed together into one supercontinent, Pangea. What is now West Texas sat near its edge, close to the equator. And here, the crust was sagging. A wide hollow formed, and the ocean flowed in through a narrow channel. The U.S. Geological Survey describes it as an inland sea, connected to the vast ocean around Pangea by that one passage.

P4: The sea had a shape you can still trace today. In the west was a deep basin, now called the Delaware Basin. In the east, a second one, the Midland Basin. And between them, a raised block of crust, the Central Basin Platform, where the water was shallow. The whole region took its name from the period, and from the rocks laid down in it: the Permian Basin.

P5: In warm, shallow water near the surface, life was everywhere. Sponges, algae and small shelled animals grew in enormous numbers. When they died, many of them sank into the deep parts of the basins. And down there, the water barely moved. The USGS explains that the burial and decay of all those dead organisms used up the oxygen in the deep water. Without oxygen, nothing finished eating them. The remains were simply buried, layer after layer, in dark mud.

P6: Over millions of years, that mud was pressed under more and more rock, and slowly heated. Heat and pressure turned the buried life into oil and gas. That is the first part of the answer. The oil under West Texas is the remains of a sea's worth of living things, buried in a basin that was built to keep them.

P7: But a basin full of oil is not enough. Oil is light. Left alone, it creeps upward through the rock and escapes. For a field to last two hundred and fifty million years, something has to hold it in. And around the edge of this sea, something was growing that would help do exactly that.

## 2. The Reef That Became a Mountain (curious)
P8: Along the rim of the deep Delaware Basin, sponges, algae and other organisms built a reef. The USGS dates it to between two hundred and seventy-seven and two hundred and seventy-five million years ago. Geologists call it the Capitan Reef. It grew in a long U-shape around the basin, and according to the National Park Service, the whole reef complex is about four hundred miles long. In places, it is up to eighteen hundred feet thick.

P9: Most of that reef is still buried. But in one place, you can walk on it. About eighty million years ago, the USGS says, the whole region began to rise. Later, faults lifted one block of the reef thousands of feet into the air. Today that block is the Guadalupe Mountains, near the New Mexico line. The highest point, Guadalupe Peak, stands eight thousand seven hundred and fifty-one feet above sea level. It is the highest point in Texas.

P10: At the southern end of the range is a sheer pale cliff called El Capitan. It is part of the same reef. The highest ground in Texas was built by sponges and algae at the bottom of a sea. The exposed stretch of the reef runs about forty miles from the national park northeast, almost to the town of Carlsbad, New Mexico.

P11: And under Carlsbad, the reef is hollow. Carlsbad Cavern runs for more than thirty miles. Nearby Lechuguilla Cave, also inside the reef, has been mapped for more than a hundred and thirty-eight miles. Most caves are carved slowly by rainwater. These were not. According to the National Park Service, they were dissolved by sulfuric acid.

P12: Here is how. Hydrogen sulfide gas rose from the oil and gas deposits deep below. The USGS says this was happening as late as about twelve million years ago. When the gas met groundwater carrying oxygen, the two made sulfuric acid, and the acid ate through the limestone of the reef. The proof is a mineral left on the cave floors: gypsum, which forms when that acid reacts with limestone. So one of America's most famous caves was carved by the oil field next door.

P13: The reef ringed the deep basin, and the oil formed in the deep basin. But the reef could not seal anything on its own. What finally locked the oil in place came at the end, when the sea itself began to die.

## 3. The Sea Dies (tense)
P14: Toward the end of the Permian, the narrow channel that fed this sea was gradually cut off. Fresh seawater could no longer flow in easily. And under the hot sun near the equator, the water kept evaporating. As it shrank, it grew saltier and saltier, until the salt began to fall out of the water and settle on the bottom.

P15: Layer after layer of salt and gypsum piled up. Two formations hold most of it, the Castile and, above it, the Salado. The National Academies describe the Salado alone as two hundred to four hundred metres of beds, mostly rock salt. That is up to thirteen hundred feet of salt, left behind by a sea that dried in place.

P16: Salt does something unusual. Under pressure, it slowly flows, like a very thick paste, and it closes cracks around it. A thick blanket of salt over a basin works like a lid. Together with the dense rocks around the edge, it helped hold the oil and gas down for a quarter of a billion years.

P17: The end of the Permian was not just the end of this sea. About two hundred and fifty-two million years ago, the period closed with the largest mass extinction known. Britannica estimates that about ninety percent of all species on Earth disappeared, including more than ninety-five percent of marine species. It is often called the Great Dying. The sea that made West Texas was gone, and so was most of the life that had filled the world's oceans.

P18: That salt is still there. Near Carlsbad, the U.S. Department of Energy runs the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant, which takes radioactive waste from the country's nuclear weapons program. Its disposal rooms are dug into the Salado salt, about two thousand one hundred and fifty feet underground. The Department of Energy says the salt was laid down about two hundred and fifty million years ago, as the Permian Sea evaporated, and that its most important quality is the way salt seals fractures and closes openings. The same property that kept the oil down is now trusted to keep nuclear waste in place.

P19: So by the end of the Permian, everything was ready. A thick layer of buried life, cooked into oil. A reef along the edge. And salt over the top. Then the oil waited for about two hundred and fifty million years, until someone came out to this empty country with a drilling rig.

## 4. The First Boom (warm)
P20: Texas wrote a new constitution in 1876, and it gave its future state university one million acres of land in the dry west. In 1883, the legislature added another million acres, land a railroad had handed back because it was considered too worthless even to survey. Historians have called the first grant much less desirable land in arid western Texas. For decades, nobody disagreed.

P21: Then, on the twenty-eighth of May, 1923, a well called Santa Rita Number One came in about thirteen miles west of the town of Big Lake, in Reagan County. It stood on university land. According to the University of Texas System, it was the first major oil discovery in the Permian Basin. Its rig now stands on the university's campus in Austin.

P22: That well changed what the university's desert was worth. Today, the Permanent University Fund owns about two point one million acres, mostly in West Texas. In fiscal year 2025, oil and gas from those lands brought in about one point seven billion dollars, according to University Lands. And the fund itself was worth about thirty-nine and a half billion dollars in mid-2025. A sea that died two hundred and fifty million years ago now helps pay for universities in Texas.

P23: Three years after Santa Rita, in October 1926, an even bigger strike came in Pecos County, on a ranch owned by Ira and Ann Yates. The state historical marker says the discovery well was rated at more than seventy-two thousand barrels a day, from a depth of just eleven hundred and fifty feet. The town that grew up beside the field took its name from the couple: Iraan. The Yates field has since produced more than five hundred million barrels.

P24: Through the following decades, oil money built Midland and Odessa in the middle of the basin. Midland became the office town. By 1929, according to the Dallas Fed, thirty-six oil companies had opened offices there. Odessa, closer to the fields, became the working town of oilfield service companies, machinery and crews. And production kept rising, until 1973. According to the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas, Permian oil production began to decline that year, and kept declining for decades.

P25: Then prices fell too. In the 1980s, oil crashed to around ten dollars a barrel. The FDIC, the agency that insures American bank deposits, counts four hundred and twenty-five Texas commercial banks that failed between 1980 and 1989, including nine of the state's ten largest bank holding companies. The oil boom had made West Texas. The bust nearly broke it.

P26: For years after that, the Permian looked like an old field. The easy oil had been found. The wells were aging. The rock that held the most oil of all was still down there, but nobody could make it flow. Who would make it flow, and what would it cost the ground above?

## 5. The Second Boom (tense)
P27: Remember the dark mud at the bottom of the sea, the source of all that oil? Much of the oil never left it. It stayed trapped inside the mud itself, now hard shale, in pores far too small for oil to flow through on its own. One of the richest of those layers is called the Wolfcamp.

P28: Two techniques changed that. The first is horizontal drilling. A well goes straight down, then turns and runs sideways through the oil-bearing layer for a mile or more. The second is hydraulic fracturing: water, sand and chemicals pumped down at high pressure to crack the rock open, with the sand holding the cracks apart so the oil can flow.

P29: In the Permian, that combination took off fast. In May 2009, according to the Dallas Fed, just ninety-two drilling rigs were working in the basin. By June 2012, there were five hundred and twenty-five. Horizontal wells took over. By 2018, ninety percent of rigs in the basin were drilling horizontally. By 2022, ninety-five percent.

P30: That's why the desert looks like graph paper from orbit. Every pad is a cluster of wells, and from each pad, horizontal wells run out under the ground in long parallel lines. The grid you see from space is the top of a much bigger grid below.

P31: In 2017, according to an analysis by IHS Markit, the Permian produced more oil in a year than it had in its old record year of 1973. And it kept climbing. By 2025, the EIA put the basin at six point six million barrels a day. That is close to half of all U.S. crude oil, and the United States set a national record that year at thirteen point six million barrels a day.

P32: How much is left? In 2018, the USGS assessed just the Wolfcamp and Bone Spring layers of the Delaware Basin. It estimated forty-six point three billion barrels of oil that has not been discovered yet but could be recovered with today's technology. It called this the largest continuous oil and gas resource it had ever assessed. That is an estimate of what may be there, not oil that is proven and ready to pump. Still, the dead sea is far from used up.

P33: But pulling oil out of a sea floor means pulling out the sea as well. And the bill for that is showing up across the basin.

## 6. The Bill (somber)
P34: The Permian Sea is still down there, in a way. Mixed with the oil comes salty water, trapped in the rock for millions of years. In the Midland Basin, the Bureau of Economic Geology at the University of Texas puts it at about two point six barrels of water for every barrel of oil. In the Delaware Basin, about five. Across the basin, estimates for 2025 run from about twenty-one to twenty-five million barrels of water a day.

P35: That water is too salty to use as it is. Most of it is pumped back underground, into disposal wells. And that is where the ground starts to move. A member of the Railroad Commission of Texas, the state's oil and gas regulator, wrote in 2022 that seismologists had found the injection of water into the deeper formations likely responsible for the recent earthquakes in the Permian Basin.

P36: On the sixteenth of November, 2022, a quake struck near Mentone, the only community in Loving County. At the 2020 census, the whole county had sixty-four residents, the fewest of any county in the United States. The USGS measured it at magnitude five point four. It was felt as far away as El Paso, and reports called it the third-largest earthquake in Texas history. The Railroad Commission now acts when quakes of magnitude three point five or more strike, and it has already ordered deep disposal suspended in at least one area.

P37: The second cost you can see from space. The oil comes up mixed with natural gas, and where there are not enough pipelines to carry the gas away, it is burned at the wellhead in tall flares. Satellite instruments run by NOAA pick these flares up at night. A peer-reviewed study estimated that Permian flaring had grown about fourfold since 2012, and averaged about five point nine billion cubic metres of gas a year in the period it studied. That's gas burned in the open air, with no use at all.

P38: And the third cost brings us back to the salt. On the third of June, 1980, near the town of Wink, the ground collapsed into a hole about a hundred and ten feet deep. Wink Sink sits right above the buried Capitan Reef. Geologists traced it to the Salado salt below. Water had reached the salt, dissolved a cavity, and the roof had fallen in. A 1989 study concluded that the collapse was likely influenced by oil activity: an old well, drilled in 1928, stood inside the rim of the hole, and may have let water run down to the salt.

P39: In 2002, a second sinkhole opened nearby. Using radar satellites, researchers found that the ground around both holes was still sinking years later, and that one patch about a kilometre east of the second hole was sinking by more than sixty centimetres a year in 2015 and 2016. The salt that sealed the oil for two hundred and fifty million years is now dissolving under the oil field.

## 7. Ending (reflective)
P40: Fly over the Permian Basin again, and every part of it now makes sense. The empty desert is the floor of a sea. The highest mountain in Texas is that sea's reef. The caves under Carlsbad were carved by its oil. The salt is its last water, dried in place. And the grid of pale squares is people pulling that sea back out, about six point six million barrels of it every day.

P41: The USGS thinks tens of billions of barrels may still be down there. The water, the quakes and the sinkholes say the ground is starting to answer back. So here is the question I'll leave you with: how long should Texas keep pumping its dead sea? Tell me in the comments.
