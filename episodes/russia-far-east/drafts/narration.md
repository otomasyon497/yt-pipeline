# Why Russia's Pacific Coast Is Almost Empty — narration draft (sourced: research.md)

## Hook (curious)
P0: In January, on the seafront in Nice, people sit outside in the sun under palm trees. The average temperature that month is about nine and a half degrees Celsius. Now go east along the same line of latitude, a little to the south of it in fact, across Europe and Asia, all the way to the Pacific. You arrive in Vladivostok, the biggest city on Russia's Pacific coast. Its average January temperature is about minus twelve.

P1: The bay on the west side of the city freezes so thick that people walk out on it with drills and folding stools, and fish through holes in the ice. Same latitude as the French Riviera. Twenty-one degrees colder. And north of Vladivostok begins the largest and emptiest part of Russia.

P2: The Far Eastern Federal District covers almost seven million square kilometres. That is about forty percent of Russia, an area bigger than India and Argentina put together. At the last census, fewer than eight million people lived in all of it. More people than that live in the city of London alone.

P3: So why is Russia's Pacific coast almost empty? Part of the answer is in the air above it, and part is in the ground. Part of it is in two treaties, a railway built twice, and a system of camps. And part of it happened very recently. Since the Soviet Union ended, some regions here have lost two out of every three people they had. To see why, we have to start with the wind.

## 1. The Wind from Siberia (calm)
P4: Look at where Vladivostok sits. Nice has the Mediterranean to the south and the Atlantic to the west, and in winter the wind that reaches it has mostly crossed water. Vladivostok has the Pacific in front of it, but the winter wind does not come from the Pacific. It comes from behind the city, out of the middle of Asia.

P5: Every winter, the land of Siberia and Mongolia loses its heat. The days are short, the ground is covered in snow, and the snow reflects what little sunlight there is. The air over it gets very cold and very heavy, and it piles up into a huge dome of high pressure. Meteorologists call it the Siberian High. It is one of the strongest weather systems on the planet in winter, and it sits there for months.

P6: Air flows out of a high toward lower pressure, and in winter the lower pressure is over the warmer Pacific. So for months on end, the wind blows from the frozen interior out to the coast. This is the winter half of the East Asian monsoon. The same pattern that brings summer rain to China and Japan brings bitter, dry continental air down onto Russia's Pacific shore in winter.

P7: How cold is that air at its source? Inland, in the Republic of Sakha, also called Yakutia, there are two small towns that compete for the title of the coldest inhabited place in the Northern Hemisphere. In eighteen ninety-two, Verkhoyansk recorded about minus sixty-eight degrees. In nineteen thirty-three, the weather station at Oymyakon recorded minus sixty-seven point seven.

P8: And then summer comes, and the same land swings the other way. On the twentieth of June, twenty twenty, Verkhoyansk reached thirty-eight degrees. The World Meteorological Organization checked that reading and accepted it as the highest temperature ever recorded in the Arctic. One town, a range of more than a hundred degrees between its coldest and its hottest day.

P9: Nice gets its winter from the sea. Vladivostok gets its winter from Yakutia. And the cold does not stop at the shore. A few hundred kilometres to the north, it does something to the sea itself.

## 2. A Sea That Freezes (curious)
P10: North of Japan, between the Russian mainland, the long island of Sakhalin and the Kamchatka Peninsula, is the Sea of Okhotsk. It covers more than one and a half million square kilometres, almost three times the size of mainland France. And every winter, much of it freezes.

P11: According to NASA's Earth Observatory, at the annual peak, ice can cover between fifty and ninety percent of the sea. On average it stays for about a hundred and eighty days, half the year. NASA calls it the lowest latitude in the world where sea ice forms every season. The sea reaches south to about forty-five degrees north, the latitude of Venice.

P12: Why does it freeze? NASA names three reasons. The winds from East Siberia, the same outflow we just followed. Frigid air temperatures. And a large amount of fresh water pouring in from rivers, most of it from the Amur. Fresh water floats on top of salt water and freezes more easily, so the great river spreads a lid across the sea that the cold can turn to ice.

P13: The ice then drifts south. Between mid-January and early February, it usually reaches the waters off Abashiri, a port on the northeast coast of Hokkaido in Japan.

P14: Now think about what this means for a coast. A frozen sea is no use for shipping for months, and the cold water keeps the spring and summer cool, foggy and short. Along the northern shores of the Sea of Okhotsk, the coastline runs for thousands of kilometres with only a handful of towns. The biggest is Magadan. We will come back to Magadan, because what happened there tells the whole story of this region. But first, look at what is under the snow.

## 3. Ground That Never Thaws (calm)
P15: Over much of the Far East, the ground below the surface stays frozen all year round. This is permafrost: soil, rock and ice that have stayed below zero for at least two years in a row, and in much of Siberia for many thousands of years. In summer only the top layer thaws. Below it, the ground stays as hard as concrete.

P16: That sounds solid, until you heat it. A house warms the ground beneath it. The ice in the soil melts, the ground sinks unevenly, and walls crack. A road, a pipe or a rail line does the same. So in the far north, buildings are often raised on piles driven deep into the frozen ground, with a gap underneath so the cold air can keep the soil frozen.

P17: The capital of Yakutia, Yakutsk, had about three hundred and fifty-five thousand people at the last census. It is described as the largest city built on continuous permafrost. The river Lena flows past it, and there is still no bridge across the river there. Cars cross on the ice in winter and by ferry in summer.

P18: So the climate keeps people away, the sea is frozen half the year, and the ground fights everything you build. And yet Russia came here, and stayed, and built a great port at Vladivostok. Which raises a different question. If the land was this hard, why did Russia want it so much?

## 4. Taken Before It Was Settled (tension)
P19: Russian explorers and fur traders crossed Siberia in the sixteen hundreds and reached the Pacific coast by the sixteen-forties. But the southern part of this coast, the part with the mildest climate and the best harbours, was not Russian at all. It belonged, at least on paper, to the Qing dynasty of China. The border ran far to the north, along mountains.

P20: That changed in two steps, in two years. In eighteen fifty-eight, China's Qing government was fighting a rebellion at home and a war with Britain and France. The Russian governor of eastern Siberia, Nikolay Muravyov, used the moment. At the town of Aigun, on the twenty-eighth of May, eighteen fifty-eight, he and a Qing official signed a treaty. Russia received the land between the Stanovoy Mountains and the Amur River, by most estimates more than six hundred thousand square kilometres.

P21: Two years later, British and French troops had taken Beijing. A Russian envoy, Nikolay Ignatyev, offered to help negotiate, and in November eighteen sixty, a second agreement, the Convention of Peking, gave Russia the coast east of the Ussuri River, down to the border of Korea. That is today's Primorsky Krai. China calls these the unequal treaties, and the land they gave away is sometimes called Outer Manchuria.

P22: In July eighteen sixty, a few months before that second treaty was signed, a Russian military ship landed soldiers on a bay at the southern tip of this coast. They set up a post and named it Vladivostok. Roughly translated, the name means Ruler of the East. Russia now had a Pacific harbour much further south than any it had before. But it was still almost empty, and it was more than six thousand kilometres from Moscow, even in a straight line.

P23: The answer was a railway. Work on the Trans-Siberian began in the eighteen-nineties, and it linked Moscow to Vladivostok. Look at the Far East at night today, from space, and you can still see where it runs. A thin chain of lights, tucked along the southern edge of the region close to the Chinese border. Khabarovsk, about six hundred and seventeen thousand people. Vladivostok, about six hundred thousand. A few cities along the line, and north of them, darkness. Most people in the Far East still live close to that one railway.

P24: The gold, the timber, the coal and the fish were far to the north, in the dark part of the map. Few people would move there for an ordinary wage. So in the twentieth century, the Soviet Union tried two other ways of filling it. The first was force.

## 5. Filled by Force (tension)
P25: In November nineteen thirty-one, the Soviet government created a trust called Dalstroy, the Far North Construction Trust. Its job was to dig gold out of the valley of the Kolyma River, in the far northeast. There were no roads and almost no people. Dalstroy turned the small settlement of Magadan, on the Sea of Okhotsk, into its port, and from there it pushed a road inland across the mountains and the permafrost.

P26: That road became the Kolyma Highway, about two thousand kilometres long, built between nineteen thirty-two and the early nineteen-fifties. Most of the people who built it, and who worked the gold mines it led to, were prisoners of the Gulag, the Soviet system of labour camps. By one archival count, in nineteen fifty-two the camps under Dalstroy held close to two hundred thousand prisoners.

P27: How many died in Kolyma is still argued over. Estimates by historians range from around a hundred and thirty thousand to more than a million. We do not know the true number, and we are not going to pretend we do. What is not in dispute is the name people gave the highway. They called it the Road of Bones.

P28: After Stalin died in nineteen fifty-three, the camps emptied, and in nineteen fifty-seven Dalstroy itself was dissolved. Some former prisoners stayed, because they had nowhere else to go. So the state turned to the second way. Money.

## 6. Filled by Money (curious)
P29: For decades, the Soviet Union paid people to go north and east. Workers in the Far North and other remote zones received wage bonuses on top of their pay, cost-of-living extras and earlier pensions, and they were guaranteed housing. For many people, that was the deal: go to the edge of the country, work hard, save, and come back richer.

P30: And the state was still building. In March nineteen seventy-four, it revived an old plan for a second line across Siberia: the Baikal-Amur Mainline, or BAM. It would run about four hundred miles north of the Trans-Siberian, further from the Chinese border, across seven mountain ranges, swamps and taiga. About forty percent of its track was laid on permafrost. The full line runs for more than four thousand three hundred kilometres.

P31: The BAM was sold as the project of a generation. Young volunteers from across the Soviet Union came to build it, and new towns grew along the route. In nineteen eighty-four, a golden spike joined the eastern and western sections. But at that point, less than a third of the line was actually working. The line was cut back to a single track as the money ran out, and it was only declared complete in nineteen ninety-one.

P32: Nineteen ninety-one. The same year the Soviet Union came apart. So now picture all of these places at that moment. Mining towns on the permafrost, railway towns in the taiga, ports on a frozen sea, all built by a state that paid people extra to live there and shipped in their food and fuel. And then, at the end of nineteen ninety-one, that state stopped existing.

## 7. The Great Emptying (tension)
P33: What happened next shows up very clearly in the census. In nineteen eighty-nine, the last Soviet census counted three hundred and eighty-five thousand people in Magadan Oblast, the region around the city of Magadan. At Russia's census of twenty twenty-one, it had about a hundred and thirty-six thousand. That is a loss of sixty-five percent. Almost two out of every three people gone.

P34: Chukotka, the far northeastern tip of Russia across the strait from Alaska, had about a hundred and fifty-eight thousand people in nineteen eighty-nine. In twenty twenty-one: about forty-seven thousand. That is seventy percent gone. Kamchatka lost more than a third of its people. So did Sakhalin. Even the southern Primorsky Krai, with Vladivostok in it, lost close to a fifth.

P35: Why so fast? When the bonuses lost their value and the state stopped paying for food, fuel and flights, life in a place like this became very expensive. The mines and factories that had been kept running for strategic reasons closed. People who could leave, left. Some mining settlements were abandoned completely, and in many towns, whole apartment blocks stand empty.

P36: The Far East is empty partly because nature made it hard. But it was emptier in twenty twenty-one than it had been in nineteen eighty-nine. Most of those people did not leave because the climate changed. They left because the reason they had come was gone.

P37: Russia has tried to reverse it. In twenty sixteen, it launched a programme known as the Far Eastern Hectare. Russian citizens can apply for a free plot of land here, about one hectare, to build a house, start a farm or run a business. According to the state corporation that runs it, by August twenty twenty-five, about a hundred and fifty-one thousand people had received a plot in the Far East. Just over half of the land is used for housing, and about a quarter for farming.

P38: That sounds like a lot until you set it against the losses. Magadan and Chukotka alone lost more than three hundred and fifty thousand people. So here is a question for you. If you were offered a free hectare of land anywhere in Russia's Far East, would you take it, and where would you put it? Tell me in the comments. Because before you answer, there are two more things you should know about this coast.

## 8. The Edge of the Plate, and the Edge of China (curious)
P39: The first is under the sea. On the twenty-ninth of July, twenty twenty-five, at twenty-four minutes past eleven at night, universal time, the seafloor off the coast of Kamchatka moved. The U.S. Geological Survey measured it at magnitude eight point eight. It is among the ten largest earthquakes recorded anywhere since nineteen hundred, and the largest on Earth since the Tōhoku earthquake in Japan in twenty eleven. Tsunami warnings went out across the Pacific.

P40: It happened because the Pacific plate is sliding under this coast at about eighty millimetres a year, one of the fastest collisions between plates in the world. That same push lifts the volcanoes of Kamchatka and the Kuril Islands. In nineteen fifty-two, the same stretch of coast produced a magnitude nine earthquake. Russia's Pacific edge is not only cold. It is some of the most restless ground on the planet.

P41: The second thing is across a river. Upstream from Khabarovsk, the Amur and its branch, the Ussuri, form a long stretch of the border between Russia and China. On the Russian side, the whole Far Eastern Federal District has fewer than eight million people. On the Chinese side, the three northeastern provinces of Heilongjiang, Jilin and Liaoning had about ninety-eight and a half million people at China's census of twenty twenty. Heilongjiang alone, the province right across the river, has about four times as many people as the entire Russian Far East.

P42: But those Chinese provinces are emptying too. At their census, all three held a smaller share of China's people than ten years before. Analyses of that census found that many people had moved away, to the richer south. On both sides of the Amur, the cold north of Asia is losing people to the places where life is easier.

## Ending (calm)
P43: So why is Russia's Pacific coast almost empty? Start with the wind. Every winter, some of the coldest air in the Northern Hemisphere pours out of Siberia onto a coast that, on a map, looks like it should be as mild as southern Europe. That air freezes the Sea of Okhotsk closer to the equator than sea ice forms anywhere else on Earth, and it keeps the ground frozen underneath the towns.

P44: Then add history. Russia took the southern coast by treaty, before it had people to put on it. It filled the north first with prisoners, then with wages, and when the wages ended, the people went home. What is left is a thin line of cities along one railway, and behind it, an area bigger than India with fewer people than London.

P45: Vladivostok and Nice still sit at the same latitude. One faces a warm sea with the wind at its back. The other faces the Pacific with the frozen heart of Siberia behind it. A free hectare of land cannot change which way the wind blows. The open question is whether anything Russia can offer will make people choose to live here, now that no one is ordering them to.
