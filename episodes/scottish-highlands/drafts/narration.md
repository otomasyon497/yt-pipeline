# Why Almost Nobody Lives in the Scottish Highlands — narration draft (sourced: research.md)

## Hook (curious)

P0: Look at the north of Scotland on a map. The Highland council area alone is bigger than Wales, about twenty-five and a half thousand square kilometres of mountains, glens and sea lochs. About two hundred and thirty-five thousand people live there. That is roughly nine people for every square kilometre. Among Scotland's council areas, only the Western Isles are emptier.

P1: Now cross the North Sea to the west coast of Norway. The county of Vestland is somewhat larger, and it is made of the same ancient rock. It faces the same Atlantic storms. In fact, Bergen, its biggest city, gets almost twice as much rain as the Great Glen. Yet Vestland has more than six hundred thousand people, about twice as many to every square kilometre, and Bergen alone is home to close to three hundred thousand.

P1b: Put the two side by side and the gap is hard to miss. Vestland has about nineteen people for every square kilometre. The Highlands have nine. Both coasts are wet, steep and cut by long arms of the sea. One filled up. The other emptied.

P2: And here is the strangest part. The Highlands were not always empty. In eighteen sixty-one, the same area held more people than it does today. About a century later, more than a third of them were gone.

P3: So why did two coasts that look almost the same end up so different? The answer runs through rock and ice, through a battle on a moor near Inverness, through sheep and potatoes and ships, through hunting estates the size of small countries, and through a question that Scotland is still arguing about today. And it ends with a question for you.

## 1. Rock, Ice and Bog (calm)

P4: Start with the ground. A straight line cuts across Scotland on any relief map, from the Isle of Arran in the west to Stonehaven on the north-east coast. Geologists call it the Highland Boundary Fault. It runs for more than two hundred and forty kilometres. South of it lies the Midland Valley, the lower, gentler country where Glasgow and Edinburgh grew. North of it, the land rises into the Highlands.

P4b: That one line still divides Scotland's people. Scotland has eight cities. Six of them lie south of the fault, in or near the Midland Valley. Only two, Aberdeen and Inverness, lie north of it, and both sit on the lower, drier east coast.

P5: The rocks up here are old and hard. They are the worn-down roots of a mountain chain that once ran from Norway through Scotland to North America. Then, during the ice ages, glaciers ground across them again and again. They carved the deep glens and the long lochs, and they scraped much of the soil away. What the ice left behind was bare rock, boulders and thin, stony ground.

P6: In the cool, wet climate that followed, dead plants did not rot away. They piled up as peat, layer on layer, for thousands of years. In the far north, in Caithness and Sutherland, the peat spreads into one of the largest blanket bogs in the world. It is called the Flow Country. In July twenty twenty-four, UNESCO made about one hundred and ninety thousand hectares of it the first peatland World Heritage Site. And that is only about half of the whole bog, which covers some four hundred thousand hectares.

P7: You cannot plough a bog. But in the nineteen-seventies and eighties, people tried to make money from it anyway. Tax breaks for commercial forestry made it worthwhile for wealthy investors to drain the bog and plant rows of Sitka spruce. The RSPB says nearly a fifth of the Flow Country was drained and planted. In March nineteen eighty-eight, the British government ended the tax relief that had paid for it, and today parts of those plantations are being cut down so the bog can recover.

P8: So the ground is poor. But poor ground alone does not explain an empty country. Norway's west coast is made of the same kind of rock, scraped by the same ice. Something else must be different.

## 2. The Rain Wall (curious)

P9: The obvious suspect is the weather. Scotland's weather comes mostly from the west, off the Atlantic. When those wet winds hit the mountains, the air is forced up, it cools, and it drops its rain. On the Met Office's long-term averages, Fort Augustus, in the middle of the Great Glen, gets about one thousand three hundred and sixty millimetres a year. Edinburgh, on the dry east side, gets about six hundred and forty.

P10: Wet summers mean a short growing season. Grain struggles to ripen, and hay is hard to dry. That is why the best farmland in the north hugs the drier east coast, around the Moray Firth and Aberdeenshire.

P11: But now look across the sea again. Bergen's official average, by the city's own climate figures, is about two thousand five hundred millimetres a year. That is nearly twice the rain of Fort Augustus and four times that of Edinburgh. And Bergen became one of the great trading cities of northern Europe.

P11b: For centuries, Bergen lived on the sea. From the fourteenth century, merchants of the Hanseatic League ran a trading office on its wharf, Bryggen, and shipped dried cod from northern Norway to the Baltic and the rest of Europe. Bryggen's wooden warehouses still stand, and UNESCO listed them as a World Heritage Site in nineteen seventy-nine.

P12: Same rock, same ice, even more rain. So the land made the Highlands hard to live in. It did not make them empty. To understand what did, we have to go back to one cold morning in seventeen forty-six.

## 3. From Clans to Cash (tension)

P13: For most of their history, the Highlands were full of people. Glens that are silent today were lined with townships, small clusters of stone houses with shared fields and cattle on the hills above. Families belonged to clans, and a chief's power was measured in the number of men he could call out to fight.

P14: On the sixteenth of April, seventeen forty-six, the Jacobite army of Charles Edward Stuart met government troops on Culloden Moor, just east of Inverness. By the National Trust for Scotland's account, hardly an hour passed between the first shots and the flight of the Prince's army. It was the last pitched battle fought on British soil.

P15: What followed was meant to make sure the clans could never rise again. The government banned Highland dress, took away the chiefs' old legal powers and pushed more military roads deep into the glens. But the biggest change was quieter. A chief without a private army no longer needed hundreds of fighting men. What he needed was money. And to a landowner short of cash, a glen full of tenants began to look like a glen full of wasted rent.

P16: The people had stopped being the chief's strength. Now they were in the way. And the animal that would replace them was already being driven north.

## 4. The Clearances (tension)

P17: From the late seventeen-hundreds, Highland landowners found that sheep paid far better than people. Sheep farmers from the south would pay high rents for whole glens. But families were already living on that land, so they were moved. Over roughly a century, in waves, this became known as the Highland Clearances.

P18: Some families were shifted to small coastal plots called crofts, often too small to live on, so that they would have to fish or gather seaweed as well. Others were simply told to leave.

P19: The most notorious removals came in Sutherland, in the far north. In Strathnaver, the estate's factor, Patrick Sellar, served notices in December eighteen thirteen, and the first removals cleared twenty-eight families. Houses were burned so that people could not return. In April eighteen sixteen, Sellar stood trial in Inverness, accused of arson and culpable homicide. He was acquitted.

P19b: The man who owned the Sutherland estates at the height of the clearances was George Granville Leveson-Gower, later the first Duke of Sutherland. After his death in eighteen thirty-three, a monument was raised to him on Ben Bhraggie above Golspie. The statue and its plinth stand about thirty metres tall, and they still look down over the coast. It is one of the most argued-over monuments in the country, and over the years vandals have tried to bring it down.

P20: Walk up Strathnaver today and you can still find the low stone outlines of those townships in the grass. In glen after glen, the same traces survive: green patches of old fields and the rings of fallen walls. Whole valleys were emptied within a single lifetime. But the clearances alone did not empty the Highlands. Something else was coming for the people who were left.

## 5. Hunger and the Ships (calm)

P21: By the eighteen-forties, many families on the crofts lived on little except potatoes. Then, in eighteen forty-six, the same blight that devastated Ireland reached the Highlands. Crops rotted in the ground, and famine spread along the west coast and through the islands.

P22: For many, the only way out was a ship. Between eighteen fifty-two and eighteen fifty-seven, one charity, the Highland and Island Emigration Society, helped four thousand nine hundred and nineteen men, women and children sail to Australia. Skye was the first place chosen. In eighteen fifty-two alone, more than two and a half thousand people left on the Society's ships.

P22b: Skye gave more than any other place. By the time the Society closed, about six in ten of all its emigrants had come from that one island.

P23: Thousands more went to Canada, often with the landlord paying the fare to clear the land. The road had been opened long before. In seventeen seventy-three, a ship called the Hector had carried close to two hundred Highlanders to Pictou, in a colony with a telling name: Nova Scotia, New Scotland.

P24: The census shows what all of this did. Measured on today's council boundaries, the Highland population peaked at about two hundred and fifty-three thousand in eighteen sixty-one. By nineteen seventy-one it had fallen to about one hundred and sixty-two thousand. That is more than a third of the people gone, while Scotland as a whole grew by about seventy percent. So when the people left, what filled the glens?

## 6. Deer Forests and St Kilda (curious)

P25: Not sheep, in the end. By the late eighteen-hundreds, wool prices were falling and many Highland sheep farms stopped paying. Landowners found a new use for the hills: hunting. Wealthy Victorians would pay well to stalk red deer, and estate after estate was turned into a deer forest, which, despite the name, usually has hardly any trees.

P26: The landowners' own returns, read out in Parliament in eighteen ninety-three, counted about one point nine eight million acres of deer forest in eighteen eighty-three. By eighteen ninety-one, it was about two point five seven million. That is nearly a third more land kept empty for sport in just eight years. A deer forest needs even fewer people than a sheep farm: a few stalkers, a gamekeeper and a lodge used for some weeks a year.

P26b: Two and a half million acres is about ten thousand square kilometres. That is roughly an eighth of Scotland's land, given over to a few weeks of stalking each autumn.

P27: The crofters fought back. In April eighteen eighty-two, on Skye, crofters at Braes faced the police over the arrest of five of their men, in what became known as the Battle of the Braes. In March eighteen eighty-three, the government appointed a royal commission under Lord Napier, which travelled the Highlands and took evidence from crofters themselves. And in June eighteen eighty-six, the Crofters Act gave crofters security of tenure at last, fair rents set by a commission and the right to pass their crofts to their heirs. They could no longer be thrown off their plots. But the act did not give them back the hills.

P28: The last emptying came by request. Far out in the Atlantic, the islanders of St Kilda had lived for centuries on seabirds, sheep and fishing. After a hard winter, they sent a petition in May nineteen thirty asking the government to move them. Many of the young had already left, and most of those who remained were older. On the twenty-ninth of August, nineteen thirty, the last thirty-six islanders were taken to the mainland. The island has had no permanent community since. Today it belongs to the National Trust for Scotland.

## 7. Norway's Different Path (curious)

P29: Now go back to the question from the start. Norway had the same hard rock, the same ice-scraped soil and even more rain. Why did its west coast keep its people?

P30: Part of the answer is who owned the land. Historians of Norway have argued that tenant farmers there were already buying their farms in the late sixteen-hundreds, and that by about seventeen-fifty freeholders may have been the majority. Norway's constitution of eighteen fourteen protected an old family right to the farm, called the odal right. A farmer who owns his land cannot be cleared to make room for sheep.

P31: In the Highlands, by contrast, almost everyone was a tenant on someone else's land. When the land became more valuable without them, they had no right to stay.

P32: Scotland did try to bring life back. In August nineteen forty-three, Parliament created the North of Scotland Hydro-Electric Board, pushed through by Tom Johnston. Its motto was Gaelic: Neart nan Glean, power from the glens. In the decades that followed, it built dozens of power stations and dams, and electricity reached crofts that had never had it. By the nineteen-seventies, the Highland population had stopped falling. But the hydro schemes made power, not farms. The land itself stayed in very few hands.

P32b: The board's engineers worked across the whole north. By most accounts, there were in the end fifty-four power stations, seventy-eight dams and some two hundred miles of tunnels. The first scheme to switch on, on the River Morar, started up in December nineteen forty-eight. Many of those dams still hold back the lochs you see in Highland postcards today.

## 8. Who Owns the Glens (tension)

P33: How few? According to the land researcher Andy Wightman, about four hundred owners hold half of all the privately owned rural land in Scotland. Some estates cover tens of thousands of acres. If you want to build a house or start a farm in a Highland glen, you often need the permission of one landowner who may have other plans.

P34: That began to change in a small way in nineteen ninety-three. In Assynt, in the far north-west, crofters bought the estate they lived on, about twenty-one thousand acres, for three hundred thousand pounds, about half of it raised from public donations. Four years later, in June nineteen ninety-seven, the residents of the Isle of Eigg became their own landlords.

P34b: Eigg was bought by a partnership of its sixty-five residents, the Highland Council and the Scottish Wildlife Trust. In two thousand and eight, the island switched on its own power grid, run on water, wind and sun. The people who had been tenants for centuries were now deciding what happened to their own land.

P35: Those buyouts changed the law. The Land Reform Act of two thousand and three gave rural communities a right to buy. A community can register an interest in the land around it, and when the owner decides to sell, the community gets the first chance to buy. Since then, many more communities have bought the land they live on. And the Highland population has grown back, to about two hundred and thirty-five thousand in the last census.

P36: But it has not grown back to eighteen sixty-one. Young people still leave for the cities. In the prettiest villages, houses are bought as holiday homes, and prices climb beyond what local wages can pay. The deer forests are still there, and so are the big estates.

## Ending (calm)

P37: So why are the Scottish Highlands so empty? The land made it hard: old rock scraped bare by ice, smothered in peat and soaked by Atlantic rain. But Norway shows that hard land does not empty itself. In the Highlands, the people never owned the ground they lived on. After Culloden, they became a cost. Sheep replaced them, famine and ships took them away, and deer forests kept the hills empty for sport.

P38: Scotland is still debating how far to go. In recent years, its government has proposed new rules for the largest estates, including splitting them into smaller lots when they are sold. Supporters say breaking them up is the only way to bring people back to the glens. Opponents say the land is too poor to support many more farms, and that the estates pay for jobs and conservation.

P39: So here is the question I will leave you with. Should Scotland break up the big Highland estates to bring people back to the glens, or keep the hills as they are? Tell me in the comments.
