## Hook (curious)

P0: Namibia's Skeleton Coast and the desert shore south of it run for more than thirteen hundred kilometres along the Atlantic. That is about as long as the whole coast of California.
[ON SCREEN: painted map of Africa, Namibia's coast red; the coastline draws itself]

P1: But almost nobody lives on it. We counted where Namibians live, and only about one in fourteen lives within fifty kilometres of the sea.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 dunes meeting the surf; stat card "7 %" (M1)]

P2: So why does almost nobody live on Namibia's Skeleton Coast?
[ON SCREEN: question card over the red coast]

P3: The answer runs through a harbour that belonged to another country until 1994, a rescue that wrecked a tug and a bomber, and a diamond town that was closed to outsiders until 2017.
[ON SCREEN: Walvis Bay from orbit; Dunedin Star wreck photo; Oranjemund gate photo]

P4: The first reason is the sea itself.
[ON SCREEN: map, the Benguela current drawn as a blue arrow up the coast]

## 1. A Sea That Will Not Rain (calm)

P5: Reason one. The ocean off this coast is cold, and cold water does not make rain.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, Benguela current arrow; Namibia coast red]

P6: A current called the Benguela carries water up from the south, and winds lift colder water from the deep to the surface. The air above it is chilled from below. It stays low and heavy, and the clouds that would bring rain never build.
[ON SCREEN: map steps: arrow north, label "Benguela current"; Sentinel-2 of fog banks over the coast]

P7: Walvis Bay, the biggest town on the coast, gets about half an inch of rain a year. Windhoek, the capital, up on the plateau, gets more than twenty times as much.
[ON SCREEN: chart "Rain a year": Walvis Bay ~13 mm, Windhoek ~360 mm]

P8: What the sea does give is fog. On many mornings it rolls inland, and the beetles and plants of the Namib live on the drops it leaves. People cannot.
[ON SCREEN: photo of fog over the dunes (CC BY); photo of a Welwitschia or fog beetle]

P9: Behind the beach lies the Namib, a desert of gravel plains and some of the tallest dunes on earth. In many places they run straight into the sea.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 of the dune sea meeting the coast south of Walvis Bay]

P9b: South of Walvis Bay the sand sea is about three hundred and twenty kilometres long. Its northern edge is a dry river, the Kuiseb, and the dunes stop at its bed as if at a wall.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 of the Kuiseb with red dunes to the south and grey gravel plains to the north]

P10: Rivers cross it rarely. Most of them reach the coast only after heavy rain far inland, and some years not at all.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 of the Kuiseb river bed stopping at the dunes]

P11: A coast with no rain, no rivers and no farmland gave people no reason to stop. And the first Europeans who saw it did not stop either.
[ON SCREEN: map pull-back to the whole coast]

## 2. No Harbour, No Water (calm)

P12: Reason two. Ships need water and a safe harbour, and this coast had almost neither.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, Cape Cross and Walvis Bay labelled]

P13: In January 1486, the Portuguese captain Diogo Cão landed at a headland north of today's Swakopmund and set up a stone cross to claim it. Then he sailed on.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the Cape Cross replica cross (CC BY); stat card "1486"]

P13b: The headland is still called Cape Cross. Today it belongs to seals. In the breeding season up to two hundred and ten thousand Cape fur seals crowd its rocks, the largest colony of its kind in the world.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the Cape Cross seal colony (CC BY); stat card "210,000 SEALS"]

P14: That cross stood there for four hundred years. In 1893 a German naval officer took it to Berlin, where it stood in a museum for more than a century. Namibia asked for it back in 2017, and in 2019 it was shipped home.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the original padrão in the German Historical Museum (if licensed) or map arrow to Berlin]

P14b: For four centuries after Cão, sailors mostly passed by. There was nothing on the shore to stop for.
[ON SCREEN: map, ship tracks passing offshore]

P15: Along the whole coast there was one good natural harbour: Walvis Bay, a deep bay sheltered by a sandspit.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 Walvis Bay and Pelican Point spit]

P16: Britain claimed it in 1878, and in 1884 made it part of its Cape Colony. That same year, Germany claimed the land all around it.
[ON SCREEN: map: Walvis Bay enclave red, German South West Africa outlined; stat card "1878"]

P16b: Germany's claim began with a merchant from Bremen, Adolf Lüderitz. In May 1883 he bought a sheltered cove in the south, Angra Pequena, and five miles of land around it from a Nama chief, Josef Frederiks, for a hundred pounds in gold and two hundred rifles.
[ON SCREEN: photo of Lüderitz town and bay (CC BY); stat card "£100 + 200 RIFLES"]

P16c: A second contract that August took a strip of land to the Orange River, measured in miles. The chief understood English miles. The contract meant German geographical miles, almost five times longer. It became known as the mile swindle.
[ON SCREEN: map, the strip drawn twice: short and long]

P16d: Lüderitz went to look for minerals on the Orange River in 1886 and never came back. The cove was renamed after him: Lüderitz.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 Lüderitz bay]

P17: So the Germans had a colony with a coast, but the only good harbour on that coast was British.
[ON SCREEN: map push-in on the enclave]

P18: They tried to make one of their own at Swakopmund, thirty kilometres to the north. In 1903 they opened a stone pier, the Mole, to shelter the landing boats.
[ON SCREEN: archive photo of the Mole, Swakopmund (PD); Sentinel-2 of Swakopmund]

P19: Within about two years the sea had filled it with sand. Ships had to unload into small boats again, and later onto a wooden jetty.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the Swakopmund jetty (CC BY)]

P19b: They had already built a railway, to carry everything from the poor landing at Swakopmund up to the capital. The line to Windhoek, three hundred and eighty-two kilometres of narrow track, opened in June 1902.
[ON SCREEN: map, the 1902 line drawn from Swakopmund to Windhoek; stat card "382 KM, 1902"]

P19c: Swakopmund grew as a German town at the end of that line, and today it is Namibia's seaside resort. But its sea front was never a real harbour.
[ON SCREEN: photo of Swakopmund's old buildings (CC BY); Sentinel-2 of the town]

P20: The bay stayed in other hands for a long time. When the British Cape became part of South Africa in 1910, Walvis Bay went with it.
[ON SCREEN: map, enclave red, "South Africa" label]

P21: Namibia became independent in 1990. Walvis Bay did not. Its only deep-water port stayed South African for four more years, until the first of March 1994.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "1 MAR 1994"; photo of Walvis Bay harbour today (CC BY)]

P22: So for a century, the one place where a coastal city could grow was the one place the country running the coast did not own. And the rest of the coast was busy sinking ships.
[ON SCREEN: map, the long coast north of Walvis Bay]

## 3. The Coast of Wrecks (tension)

P23: Reason three. The coast is dangerous to reach even from the sea.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, the northern coast red; wreck symbols]

P24: Fog hides the shore, the surf is heavy, and sandbanks shift. Over the centuries hundreds of ships ran aground here. Their rusting hulls, with the bones of whales and seals on the beaches, gave the Skeleton Coast its name.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the Eduard Bohlen wreck in the sand (CC BY); photo of the Zeila wreck (CC BY)]

P25: The story that shows it best began on the twenty-ninth of November 1942.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "29 NOV 1942"]

P26: The British cargo ship Dunedin Star, carrying 106 people, struck a shoal off the northern coast. Her captain ran her onto the beach to save her.
[ON SCREEN: map, the wreck site near Clan Alpine Shoal labelled]

P27: Many of the passengers and crew got ashore through the surf. The beach was dry, cold at night and hundreds of kilometres from any town.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 of the empty coast at the wreck site]

P28: A rescue began from three sides. A tug, the Sir Charles Elliott, came up from Walvis Bay. It too ran aground on the coast, and two of its crew drowned.
[ON SCREEN: map arrows: tug from Walvis Bay; stat card "2 DEAD"]

P29: The air force sent a Ventura bomber to drop supplies. It landed on the beach to help, and its wheels sank into the sand, and it could not take off again.
[ON SCREEN: photo of a Lockheed Ventura (PD); map]

P29b: Its pilot, Captain Naude, and his three crew were now stranded too. The rescuers needed rescuing.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "4 AIRMEN STRANDED"]

P30: In the end, a police convoy of trucks under Captain Smith crossed the desert from Windhoek to fetch the people ashore. They reached Windhoek on Christmas Eve, almost four weeks after the wreck.
[ON SCREEN: map arrow from the coast to Windhoek; stat card "24 DEC 1942"]

P31: Every one of the ship's passengers survived. But the coast had taken a ship, a tug and a bomber in a single month. That is the coast people were trying to live on.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the wreck coast today]

P31b: Most of that northern coast is now the Skeleton Coast National Park, proclaimed in 1971. Its southern half can be driven with a permit. The northern half can only be reached by small plane, with one licensed tour company.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, the park red from the Ugab to the Kunene; stat card "1971"]

P31c: Even the park's fishing camps open only for the season. Torra Bay takes campers in December and January, and is closed the rest of the year.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 Torra Bay]

P32: And on the southern coast, it was not the sea that kept people out. It was a law.
[ON SCREEN: map flies south to Lüderitz]

## 4. Diamonds Closed the Door (tension)

P32b: Lüderitz carries a darker memory too. During the war against the Herero and Nama, from 1904 to 1908, the German army held prisoners on Shark Island in Lüderitz bay. Between September 1906 and March 1907, more than a thousand of its seventeen hundred prisoners died.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 Shark Island; stat card "1,032 OF 1,795"]

P32c: In 2021 Germany said that the killings were, from today's perspective, a genocide, and asked Namibia for forgiveness. The talks about what that means are still going on.
[ON SCREEN: photo of the Shark Island memorial (if licensed) or the island today]

P33: Reason four. In the south, the most valuable land on the coast was closed to almost everyone.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, the Sperrgebiet red]

P34: In April 1908, a railway worker named Zacharias Lewala was clearing sand from the line near Lüderitz when he picked up a shiny stone. It was a diamond.
[ON SCREEN: photo of Lüderitz (CC BY); stat card "APRIL 1908"]

P35: The desert floor was scattered with them. Within months the colonial government closed off a huge strip of the coast, about twenty-six thousand square kilometres. It was called the Sperrgebiet, the forbidden area.
[ON SCREEN: map, Sperrgebiet red; stat card "~26,000 km²"]

P36: Inside it, the mining companies built towns for their own workers. At Kolmanskop there was a hospital for up to two hundred patients, an ice factory and a ballroom, in the middle of the dunes. The hospital is said to have had one of the first X-ray machines in the southern hemisphere, and some accounts say it was used to look for stolen diamonds.
[ON SCREEN: photos of Kolmanskop buildings (CC BY)]

P37: When richer deposits were found further south, the workers moved on. By 1956 Kolmanskop was empty, left to the sand, which now fills its rooms to the windowsills.
[ON SCREEN: photos of Kolmanskop interiors full of sand (CC BY)]

P38: The new centre was Oranjemund, at the mouth of the Orange River. It was a company town. For decades nobody could go there without a permit from the mining company.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 Oranjemund and the mine terraces]

P39: Namibia made it a town in 2011, but it was only opened to the public on the twenty-first of October 2017.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "21 OCT 2017"]

P40: Today the diamonds are mined by Namdeb, owned half by the state and half by De Beers. Most of the old forbidden area is now a national park, Tsau Khaeb.
[ON SCREEN: map, the park outline; photo of the Oranjemund mine (CC BY)]

P41: The closed zone even kept a treasure safe. In 2008, mining near Oranjemund uncovered the wreck of the Bom Jesus, a Portuguese ship lost in 1533. It held more than two thousand gold and silver coins.
[ON SCREEN: photo of Bom Jesus coins (if licensed); stat card "1533"]

P41b: Under the sand the diggers found about seventeen tonnes of copper ingots and more than a hundred elephant tusks as well. Portugal gave up its claim, and the treasure stayed in Namibia.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "17 TONNES OF COPPER"]

P42: Diamonds made the southern coast rich, and they kept it nearly empty. So where do the people of the coast actually live?
[ON SCREEN: map flies north to Walvis Bay and Swakopmund]

## 5. Where the People Are (calm)

P43: Reason five. The few who do live here are packed into two towns, and those towns run on water they make and rock they dig.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, Walvis Bay and Swakopmund labelled]

P43b: We wanted to know how many Namibians really live near this coast. So we took a population map of the whole country, made from satellite images and the census, and measured each small square's distance from the sea.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, hexagon dots of population across Namibia (M1)]

P43c: About seven per cent of Namibians live within fifty kilometres of the Atlantic. Go a hundred kilometres inland and the share barely moves. Beyond the towns, almost nobody lives anywhere near this coast.
[ON SCREEN: chart: share of Namibians within 5 / 50 / 100 km of the sea (M1)]

P44: We measured how far the coastline runs without a single settled square. South of Walvis Bay, almost five hundred kilometres of coast pass with nobody living by the sea.
[ON SCREEN: map, the 481 km stretch highlighted (M2); stat card "481 KM"]

P45: North of the old diamond coast, the same is true for more than three hundred kilometres, up to the Angolan border.
[ON SCREEN: map, the 347 km stretch highlighted]

P46: Walvis Bay has about a hundred thousand people, and Swakopmund about seventy-six thousand. Together they hold most of the people on the whole coast.
[ON SCREEN: chart "People, 2023 census": Walvis Bay 102,704; Swakopmund 75,921]

P47: And they are growing fast. The region around them grew by almost sixty per cent between 2011 and 2023.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "+59 %" (R7)]

P48: One reason is the port: Walvis Bay is Namibia's door to the world, for fishing boats, ships and trucks to the interior.
[ON SCREEN: photo of Walvis Bay port (CC BY)]

P48b: In 2019 a new container terminal opened there, built on about forty hectares of land reclaimed from the bay. It more than doubled the port's capacity, to about seven hundred and fifty thousand containers a year.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 of the new terminal; stat card "750,000 TEU"]

P48c: The cold, rich sea also feeds one of Africa's big fisheries. Fishing gives about sixteen thousand direct jobs, most of them in the fish plants of Walvis Bay and Lüderitz, and hake alone gives more than ten thousand.
[ON SCREEN: photo of fishing boats in Walvis Bay (CC BY); stat card "~16,000 JOBS"]

P49: The other is uranium. Inland from Swakopmund lie some of the largest uranium mines in the world. Rössing has been digging since 1976, and in 2024 Namibia produced about an eighth of the world's uranium.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 Rössing open pit; stat card "~12 %"]

P49b: The towns drink from under a dry river. Walvis Bay's water is pumped from the sands of the Kuiseb, through a pipeline built in the 1970s, and the town has no other source. A 2021 report by the water company, cited by The Namibian, found the town using more than the aquifer can sustainably give.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 of the Kuiseb delta wellfield; stat card "NO BACKUP"]

P50: Mines and towns need water, and there is almost none. So in 2010 a plant was built on the coast to turn seawater into fresh water. It was the first large plant of its kind in sub-Saharan Africa.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 of the desalination plant site near Wlotzkasbaken; stat card "2010"]

P51: In 2025 it produced about seventeen and a half million cubic metres of water, and most of it went to the mines.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "17.6 MILLION m³"]

P51b: The plant was built for up to forty-five million cubic metres a year, and the uranium miners want a second one. The towns, meanwhile, are short of homes. Walvis Bay counts a backlog of about thirty-five thousand houses, and Swakopmund about thirty-two thousand.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 of Walvis Bay's new informal suburbs; stat card "35,000 HOMES SHORT"]

P52: So people can live on this coast. But only where a port, a mine and a plant that makes water all come together.
[ON SCREEN: map, the two towns with the mines and plant marked]

## 6. The Next Bet (tension)

P53: Reason six. The empty coast is valuable precisely because it is empty, and now someone wants to fill part of it.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, Tsau Khaeb park near Lüderitz red]

P54: Near Lüderitz, a company called Hyphen plans to make green hydrogen with sun and wind. The project covers about four thousand square kilometres inside the national park and would cost around ten billion dollars.
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 Lüderitz; stat card "~$10 BILLION"]

P55: Namibia's government has taken a stake of about a quarter. The decision on whether to build is due by the end of 2026.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "24 %"]

P56: Lüderitz badly needs work, and many of its people want the project. Conservationists warn about penguins and seabirds on the islands nearby and about building inside a park.
[ON SCREEN: photo of African penguins near Lüderitz (CC BY); photo of Lüderitz town]

P56b: And offshore there is another bet. In 2022 Shell and TotalEnergies found oil deep under the sea off the southern coast. Analysts expect the first oil around the end of this decade, and a gas pipeline is planned to come ashore near Lüderitz.
[ON SCREEN: map, the Orange Basin discoveries offshore; stat card "2022"]

P56c: If those projects go ahead, the empty south could fill with workers again, a century after the diamond rush.
[ON SCREEN: photo of Kolmanskop]

P56d: The empty coast has users of its own. In the national park, two prides of desert lions have learned to hunt on the beach, taking seals, flamingos and cormorants. They are the only lions known to live on prey from the sea.
[ON SCREEN: photo of a desert lion (CC BY); Sentinel-2 of the Hoanib mouth]

P56e: The ministry counts only about a hundred desert lions in the whole northwest. Every road and pipe on this coast passes through their range.
[ON SCREEN: stat card "~100-110 LIONS"]

P57: It is the same question the coast has always asked. Is it worth bringing people, water and power to a place that never wanted them?
[ON SCREEN: Sentinel-2 dunes and sea]

## Ending (calm)

P58: So this is why almost nobody lives on Namibia's Skeleton Coast.
[ON SCREEN: painted map, the red coast again]

P59: The cold sea gives fog instead of rain. The one good harbour belonged to someone else until 1994. Ships, tugs and even a bomber were lost on the shore. Diamonds closed the south for a century. And the towns that did grow live on a port, uranium and water made from the sea.
[ON SCREEN: the six reasons as quick steps along the coast]

P60: About one Namibian in fourteen lives near this coast. Almost all of them live in two towns, with hundreds of kilometres of empty beach on either side.
[ON SCREEN: map, the two towns, empty stretches marked]

P60b: Every attempt to settle this shore has needed something rare: a deep bay, a diamond, a mine, or a machine that makes water. Where those were missing, the coast stayed empty, and it still is.
[ON SCREEN: Kolmanskop photo, then the empty beach from orbit]

P61: Should Namibia open its empty coast to new industry, or keep it the way the desert made it?
[ON SCREEN: question card; pinned comment]
