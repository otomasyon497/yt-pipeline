# Why Uruguay Has More Cows Than People — narration draft (sourced: research.md)

## Hook (curious)

P0: Uruguay is bigger than England. It has soft green hills, mild winters, good rain, no deserts and no mountains in the way. And it has fewer than three and a half million people. England has more than fifty million.

P1: Now count the cattle. Ranchers in Uruguay declare every animal they own, every year. The last count came to almost twelve million head. That is more than three cows for every person in the country.

P2: And the people are not spread out either. We measured where Uruguayans live, using a population map built from satellite images and census data. Almost half of them live within thirty kilometres of one square in central Montevideo. Half the country lives on a fifth of one percent of its land.

P3: But here is the strange part. Across the border, in the south of Brazil, the same grass rolls on into a state called Rio Grande do Sul. It holds nearly eleven million people. So why is Uruguay so empty? The answer runs through a herd of cattle set loose four hundred years ago, a country designed in London's interest, a law that fenced the countryside, ships that sailed past to Buenos Aires, and families that shrank long before anyone else's. The first reason arrived before the people did.

## 1. The Cows Came First (calm)

P4: Reason one. In the early sixteen hundreds, this coast was the edge of the Spanish empire. On the far bank of the river, the governor of Buenos Aires, Hernando Arias de Saavedra, known as Hernandarias, had a plan for the empty land to the east.

P5: The dates usually given are 1611 and 1617. Hernandarias sent cattle across the river and let them go on the grassland. Nobody herded them. The grass was endless and the winters were mild.

P6: A century later, the herds had grown into the millions. The land the Spanish called the Banda Oriental, the eastern bank, became one vast wild ranch. Hunters rode in from Buenos Aires and from Portuguese Brazil to kill cattle for their hides, which were shipped to Europe. Often the hide was all they took.

P6b: Later came the saladeros, salting plants on the riverbanks. Their workers cut the beef into strips, salted it and dried it in the sun. The product was called tasajo. Ships carried it to Brazil and Cuba, where it fed enslaved people on the sugar and coffee plantations. It was the country's first real industry, and it needed cattle, salt and a harbour. It did not need a countryside full of people.

P7: That was the first shape of the country. Not farms and villages, but animals on open grass, and men on horseback who came and went. When Spain and Portugal began to fight over this shore, they built forts, not farms. Portugal founded Colonia del Sacramento in 1680, right across the river from Buenos Aires. Spain answered with a fortress on a fine natural harbour further east. It became a city in 1726. Its name was Montevideo.

P8: Around those forts, the crown handed out the land in huge grants. A single family could hold thousands of hectares. The cattle did the work. So when the time came to make a country, there was already a pattern. A port, a few towns, and empty pasture between them.

## 2. A Country With One Door (curious)

P8b: There was one moment when it could have gone differently. In 1815, during the wars of independence, the leader of the eastern bank, José Artigas, issued a land regulation. It ordered the land of his enemies and of the state to be divided up and given out to free Black people, to Indigenous families, to poor creoles and widows. Its most famous line says that the most unfortunate should be the most privileged. A year later, Portuguese troops invaded from Brazil. Artigas was driven into exile in Paraguay, and his land reform was abandoned. The big estates stayed.

P9: Reason two. Uruguay was not planned by Uruguayans alone. After Artigas fell, the eastern bank was ruled from Rio de Janeiro, first by Portugal and then by the new Empire of Brazil, as its Cisplatine Province. At dawn on the nineteenth of April, 1825, thirty-three exiles led by Juan Antonio Lavalleja crossed the Uruguay River from Argentina and landed on a beach called Agraciada. They raised the countryside against Brazil. Argentina joined in, and the two big neighbours went to war over the eastern bank. Neither could win. Britain, whose merchants wanted the river open for trade, sent a diplomat to broker a peace. His name was Lord Ponsonby.

P10: On the twenty-seventh of August, 1828, the two sides signed a peace convention in Rio de Janeiro. The disputed land would be neither Brazilian nor Argentine. It would be a new, independent state between them. A buffer.

P11: The new country had one real door to the world, the harbour at Montevideo. Everything that mattered ran through it. The customs house, the merchants, the banks, the government. And the countryside around it was still a place of horsemen and cattle.

P12: The young state cleared that countryside in its own way. On the eleventh of April, 1831, the army of President Fructuoso Rivera attacked the Charrúa, the Indigenous people of the plains, at a creek called Salsipuedes. Rivera's own report counted about forty dead and three hundred prisoners, who were handed out to families in Montevideo.

P13: Then the country turned on itself. From 1839 to 1851, a civil war known as the Guerra Grande, the Great War, pulled in Argentina, Brazil, Britain and France. For about eight years, Montevideo was under siege. The interior was fought over by riders from both sides, and the herds were slaughtered and stolen.

P14: When peace finally held, the railways came, built and owned mostly by British companies. The first line opened in 1869. Look at the network on a map. The lines reach out into the cattle country like the ribs of a fan, and every one of them runs back to the same place. The port. Montevideo.

P15: So the country had one door, and the rails pointed to it. But a railway can carry people inland as well as out. Why didn't they go? Because by then, the land itself had been closed.

## 3. The Fences (calm)

P16: Reason three. For two hundred years, the grass of the eastern bank had been mostly open. Boundaries were streams, stone walls, hedges of cactus. Cattle wandered, and so did the people who worked them.

P17: In the 1860s, sheep arrived in huge numbers, and with them, a demand for order. On the twenty-seventh of July, 1875, a Rural Code came into force. It was written by the ranchers' own association and passed without debate. Landowners now had to mark out their properties. From 1876, imports of fencing wire took off.

P18: Within a generation, the open range was cut into fenced estancias. And a fence changes a ranch. A ranch without fences needs many riders to keep its herds from wandering off. A fenced one needs a few hands to check the wire.

P19: Historians of rural Uruguay describe what came next. Workers who had lived on the ranches were no longer needed. Many drifted to clusters of shacks on the edges of the estates, along the roads and creeks. The ranchers' associations had a name for these places. They called them pueblos de ratas. Rat towns.

P20: The countryside became productive and almost empty at the same time. The last great revolt of the horsemen came from that countryside. In 1904, the rebel leader Aparicio Saravia led thousands of riders against the government. He was shot at the Battle of Masoller on the first of September and died nine days later. It was Uruguay's last civil war. The age of the gaucho was over.

P20b: When industry did come to the cattle, it came to the riverbanks and the port. In the 1860s, the Liebig Extract of Meat Company built a factory at Fray Bentos, on the Uruguay River. It boiled down beef into a dark extract and later canned corned beef that fed European armies. Thousands worked there, and today the site is on UNESCO's World Heritage list.
P20c: Then came the frozen-meat plants. In 1916 and 1917, the American meatpackers Swift and Armour took over plants on the Cerro, the hill across the bay from Montevideo. Cattle were raised in the empty interior, sent by rail to the capital, and turned into jobs there. The work went to the port, and so did the people.

P21: So the countryside was closed by the time Europe's great wave of emigrants set sail. And that is where the story gets its sharpest contrast.

## 4. The Ships Went to Buenos Aires (curious)

P22: Reason four. Between about 1870 and 1930, some six million Europeans crossed the Atlantic to Argentina. Italians and Spaniards above all. Argentina's immigration law of 1876 offered free lodging on arrival, free train tickets inland and public land on a moving frontier.

P23: Uruguay got far fewer. Estimates for the half century after 1880 run from under three hundred thousand to nearly six hundred thousand. And most of the newcomers who did come stayed exactly where the ship docked. In 1889, almost half the people of Montevideo had been born abroad.

P23b: Italians were the largest group, nearly half of all foreigners in the capital. Even in 1908, when the whole country was counted, about one person in six had been born abroad. Most of them lived in Montevideo.

P24: The reason was the same fence. Out in the interior there was no frontier to claim. The good land was already held, in units of thousands of hectares, by families who did not need more workers. An Italian peasant could find work in Montevideo's port and workshops. He could not find a farm.

P25: Now look across the border again. In 1824, the first German families arrived at São Leopoldo, in the forested hills of northern Rio Grande do Sul. From 1875, Italians followed, about a hundred thousand of them before the First World War, most of them to the highlands around a new colony that became the city of Caxias do Sul. They were given family-sized lots, cleared the forest and built farming villages, then towns, then cities.

P26: We split Rio Grande do Sul into its official regions and measured them with the same population map. In the southwest, on the open pampa along Uruguay's border, the old cattle country, there are about eleven people per square kilometre. In the northeast, the hills of the old colonies, there are forty-seven. In Uruguay's interior, away from the southern coast, there are about seven.

P27: So the grass is not the reason. On the Brazilian side, the cattle country is almost as empty as Uruguay's. The people are where small farmers were given land. Uruguay never had that frontier.

P28: Still, a country can fill up slowly from within, if its families are large. Uruguay's were not.

## 5. Small Families, Early (calm)

P29: Reason five. Uruguay changed its family life decades before the rest of Latin America. Demographers count it among the first countries in the region to see both death rates and birth rates fall, at least thirty years ahead of its neighbours.

P30: Part of that was a country turning secular and urban early. A large share of the people lived in one city, with jobs, schools and newspapers, far from the large rural families of the interior. In the early nineteen hundreds, under President José Batlle y Ordóñez, the state separated from the church. Divorce became legal in 1907. A few years later, a wife could end a marriage simply because she wished to.

P31: By 1950, Uruguayan women had on average about two point seven children. Most of Latin America did not reach that level until the end of the century. Brazil and Argentina grew through the twentieth century, while Uruguay's population grew slowly.

P31b: The country aged with it. In 1963, fewer than one Uruguayan in twelve was sixty-five or older. In the 2023 census it was about one in six, and the statistics office expects one in three by 2070.

P32: And the fall never stopped. By 2021, the rate was down to about one point four children per woman, far below the two needed to keep a population level. Every new generation is smaller than the last.

P33: A country with small families can still grow if people arrive. Uruguay had the opposite problem. Its people left.

## 6. The Ones Who Left (curious)

P34: Reason six. In 1973, the military took power in Uruguay. Over the next twelve years, thousands were jailed for their politics. By the time democracy returned in 1985, about three hundred and eighty thousand people had left the country. In a nation of about three million, that was more than one person in ten.

P35: Then, in 2002, a banking crisis spread from Argentina. Banks closed their doors, and between 1999 and 2002 about a hundred thousand more people left. Studies found that the ones leaving were often the ones with university degrees.

P36: They went to Buenos Aires, to Spain, to the United States. Today there are hundreds of thousands of Uruguayans abroad.

P37: The latest census, in 2023, counted three point five million people, only a little more than twelve years earlier. The national statistics office said that without immigrants, the population would have fallen. Even Montevideo is getting smaller. The capital lost about five percent of its people between the two counts, as families moved out to the coast and suburbs nearby.

P37b: Living in one corner has its risks. In 2023, after a record drought, the main reservoir for the capital, Paso Severino on the Santa Lucía River, fell to a few percent of its capacity. More than half the country drinks from that one river system. For months, the water company mixed salty water from the River Plate estuary into the supply, and people in Montevideo opened their taps to water that tasted of salt. The crisis ended only when the rains came back in late August.

P38: So the people who might have filled the interior never came, and many of those who were born there went somewhere else. But the interior is not standing still. Something is growing on the old ranches.

## Ending (calm)

P38b: Our population map shows how lopsided it has become. The six departments along the southern coast hold seven in ten Uruguayans on less than a fifth of the land. In Durazno and Flores, in the middle of the country, there are fewer than five people per square kilometre. Montevideo, the smallest department of all, holds well over a million.

P39: Drive north from Montevideo today and you will see it. Long straight rows of eucalyptus, planted where cattle used to graze. More than a million hectares of the country are now pine and eucalyptus plantations.

P39b: The sheep that once came with the fences are going too. In the late 1980s, Uruguay had about twenty-five million of them. By 2025, the flock had fallen to under five million, as pasture went to crops, cattle and trees.

P40: In April 2023, a Finnish company, UPM, started a new pulp mill at Paso de los Toros, in the middle of the country. It can produce two point one million tonnes of pulp a year, from trees grown on the old grassland. And on the hilltops, wind turbines turn. In 2024, almost all of Uruguay's electricity came from renewable sources, more than a quarter of it from wind.

P41: But planted forests and wind farms, like cattle ranches, cover a great deal of land with very few workers. The interior is producing more than ever, and it is still nearly empty.

P42: The cows came first, on open grass. The country was built around one port. Fences closed the land, the ships sailed past to Buenos Aires, the families were small, and in hard times the young left. None of these alone would empty a country. Together, they made a nation that lives on its southern coast and keeps its interior for cattle, trees and wind.

P43: Would you trade a city flat for a house in Uruguay's quiet interior, with more cows than neighbours? Tell me in the comments.
