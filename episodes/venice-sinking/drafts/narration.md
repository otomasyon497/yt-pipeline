# Why Venice Is Sinking Into Its Own Lagoon — narration draft (sourced: research.md, OK rows only)

## Hook (curious)
P0: On the morning of the twenty-second of November 2022, the sea off Venice rose to one hundred and seventy-three centimeters. That is the kind of tide that, three years earlier, had put most of the city under water. This time, almost nobody in Venice got their feet wet. Somewhere under the sea, something had stood up. We will get to what it was, what it cost, and why it may not be enough.

P1: Venice is built on more than a hundred small islands in a shallow lagoon on Italy's northeast coast. The lagoon covers about five hundred and fifty square kilometers, and it meets the Adriatic Sea through just three gaps in a long strip of sand: the Lido inlet, about eight hundred meters wide, Malamocco, about four hundred, and Chioggia, a little less. For more than a thousand years, that arrangement kept the city alive.

P2: But something has gone wrong. Of all the exceptional floods recorded in Venice since 1872, the ones above one hundred and forty centimeters, more than a quarter came in just two years, 2018 and 2019. The water is higher than it used to be, and the city is lower.

P3: This is the story of how that happened: rivers that were moved by hand, wells that pulled a city down, a canal dug for oil tankers, one terrible night in 1966, and a six-billion-euro machine hidden on the seabed. Why is Venice sinking into its own lagoon, and can anything stop it? The first reason lies under the city's feet.

## 1. A City on Mud and Wood (tension)
P4: Nobody chose this place because it was easy to build on. In the fifth and sixth centuries, as the Roman world fell apart and invaders crossed northern Italy, people from the mainland towns fled into the lagoon. The marshy islands were hard to reach and hard to attack. That was the point.

P5: The ground was another problem. Under Venice lie layers of soft silt, sand and clay, laid down by rivers and tides. To build on it, the Venetians drove wooden piles into the mud, mostly oak and larch, packed close together, and set stone foundations on top. Many piles reach a firmer layer the Venetians called caranto: a stiff clay that dried out and hardened during the last ice age, between about eighteen and seven thousand years ago.

P6: The wood survives because it stays wet. Down in the waterlogged mud there is almost no oxygen, and the microbes that rot timber cannot live without it. Centuries later, much of the city still stands on those piles.

P7: But soft ground keeps settling under weight, and a lagoon is never still. The sea comes in twice a day through the three inlets. Rivers from the Alps pour fresh water and mud in from the other side. Too much mud, and the lagoon fills up and turns to land. Too much sea, and it becomes a bay. Venice needed the lagoon to stay exactly as it was. So the Venetians did something very few cities have ever tried. They moved the rivers.

## 2. They Moved the Rivers (tension)
P8: By the fifteenth century, the danger was clear. The Brenta, the Piave, the Sile and smaller rivers were dumping so much silt into the lagoon that channels were clogging and marshes were creeping toward the city. For the Republic of Venice, a lagoon full of mud meant ships that could not reach the docks and an enemy that could walk across.

P9: In 1501 the Republic created a board of three officials, the Savi alle Acque, the wise men of the waters, to run the lagoon and its rivers. A few years later a larger water council was formed, led by the Doge himself. On questions about the lagoon, the water magistrates usually had the last word, even over the mainland towns that paid the price.

P10: Then came the digging. Between 1489 and 1507, the Brenta was pushed south through a new channel. Between 1554 and 1577, the Brenta and the Bacchiglione were sent out of the lagoon altogether, to the sea near Brondolo. In 1610 work began on the Taglio Novissimo, a canal that carried the Brenta and a string of smaller rivers along the lagoon's edge. In 1579 a cut called the Taglio del Re turned the Piave east toward Caorle, and in 1664 it was diverted again. In the 1680s the Sile was led into the Piave's old bed and out to sea.

P11: It worked. The lagoon stopped filling up, and Venice stayed an island city. But the mainland paid. Diverted rivers flooded fields and villages, and new marshes spread where the water could no longer drain. And the lagoon lost something it would need later: the mud that rivers had always brought in. A lagoon without new mud can only hold its shape if nothing else takes mud out. In the twentieth century, a lot of things did.

## 3. The Wells That Sank Venice (tension)
P12: Look across the lagoon from Venice toward the mainland and you see cranes, tanks and chimneys. That is Porto Marghera, one of Italy's biggest industrial zones, built on the lagoon's edge in the twentieth century. Between 1927 and 1960, about twenty-two square kilometers of salt marsh were filled in for the factories, and the airport and new districts of Mestre took several more.

P13: Industry needs water, and the cheapest water was underground. From around the 1930s, companies drilled wells into the layers of sand beneath the lagoon and pumped out fresh groundwater. The pumping peaked between 1950 and 1970.

P14: Here is the trouble with taking water out of the ground. In soft layers of sand and clay, the water helps hold the grains apart. Pump it out, and the layers squeeze together, and everything on top goes down with them. Between 1950 and 1970, Venice sank by about twelve centimeters, in twenty years. At the peak, the city was dropping about five millimeters a year, several times faster than the slow natural settling.

P15: It took scientists and a disaster to stop it. In the early 1970s the wells were closed by law, and the sinking slowed sharply, to about a tenth of the rate of those twenty years. But the twelve centimeters never came back. Compacted clay does not spring up again. Venice was permanently lower, just as the sea was starting to rise. And Marghera had brought something else to the lagoon: ships far bigger than anything the old channels could carry.

## 4. A Canal for Oil Tankers (tension)
P16: The refineries needed tankers, and tankers needed deep water. In the 1960s, engineers dug the Malamocco-Marghera ship canal, known in Venice as the Oil Canal. It runs from the Malamocco inlet straight across the middle of the lagoon to the industrial port. You can see it from space: a dark, straight line through water that is mostly knee-deep.

P17: Digging it moved more than forty million cubic meters of sediment, and much of that was dumped on land, out of the lagoon for good. Worse, the straight, deep channel changed how water moves. Tides and waves pushed into the central lagoon with more force, stirring up the mud on the shallow flats and carrying it out to sea.

P18: Scientists who compared depth surveys of the whole lagoon from 1927, 1970 and 2002 found what that meant. The lagoon lost about one hundred and ten million cubic meters of sediment over those seventy-five years. Its typical depth went from sixty-two centimeters to eighty-eight. The area of open flats between seventy-five centimeters and two meters deep more than doubled, from eighty-eight square kilometers to two hundred and six. Salt marshes, the low green islands that break the waves, shrank from sixty-eight square kilometers to thirty-two, less than half. In the later decades, clam fishing with mechanical dredges on the lagoon bed stirred up even more mud, and the yearly loss rose from point three to point eight million cubic meters.

P19: In those surveys, the lagoon was turning into something else: deeper, more open, more like a bay of the sea. The researchers described it as starved of sediment. A deeper lagoon lets tides in faster and lets storms build bigger waves. And a city that had already sunk now sat in a lagoon that was losing its defenses. All it needed was the right storm.

## 5. The Night of 1966 (tension)
P20: On the third and fourth of November 1966, a deep low-pressure system sat over the Mediterranean, and the scirocco, the warm southeast wind, blew straight up the Adriatic for hours. It piled the sea against the top of the Adriatic, right where Venice sits. On the morning of the fourth, the sea broke through the stone sea walls at Pellestrina, the thin strip of land that shields the lagoon, in three places.

P21: The tide in Venice reached one hundred and ninety-four centimeters above the city's old reference mark, the highest ever recorded there. On the city's own flood table, that level puts about nine tenths of its streets and squares under water. Electricity failed across the whole city, hospitals included. Heating oil leaked from flooded tanks and spread through the streets and canals. More than two thousand shops and workshops were damaged, and many never reopened. That evening, the hospital on the Lido counted eight hundred people who had fled the flooded island of Pellestrina.

P22: The world noticed. In 1973 Italy passed a special law declaring the protection of Venice and its lagoon a matter of primary national interest. That law started half a century of plans, arguments and money that led, eventually, to MOSE. But the water did not wait.

P23: Venice measures every tide against a mark set at Punta della Salute in 1897. Since then the average sea level has risen to about thirty-two centimeters above that mark. A study of a century and a half of records found that roughly half of that rise came from the sea itself, and the other half from Venice sinking. Every centimeter lost on either side means the same storm floods more of the city.

P23b: The city keeps a table of what each tide does. At ninety centimeters, about two percent of the streets and squares are wet, mostly around Saint Mark's Square, the lowest part of town. At one hundred and ten, twelve percent. At one hundred and twenty, more than a quarter. At one hundred and forty, fifty-nine percent. Each step of ten centimeters floods a new ring of streets, so a city that loses ten centimeters to the sea gets every flood one step worse. In August 2020 the tide reached one hundred and three centimeters, the highest August tide since records began in 1872.

P24: Then came 2019. On the twelfth of November, four things came together. It was a spring tide, the strongest of the month. The whole northern Adriatic was running high: that November the average sea level in Venice was almost thirty centimeters above normal. The scirocco was blowing up the Adriatic. And that evening a small, intense low formed over the northern Adriatic and drove a local wind of about a hundred kilometers an hour across the lagoon, with gusts of a hundred and ten. City workers at San Zaccaria, on the waterfront, said they heard a roar just before the gust hit them. The tide reached one hundred and eighty-seven centimeters, the second highest ever recorded, and almost ninety percent of the city went under water. That year Venice had twenty-eight tides of one hundred and ten centimeters or more, the level where the sirens sound. Five tides reached one hundred and forty, and the records had never shown more than two in a single year. Three of them came within six days. In that November alone, the water stood above one hundred and ten centimeters for about fifty hours. The previous record for a November was about twenty-four. Tides once rare enough to be remembered by their dates were becoming a season.

## 6. MOSE: 78 Gates Under the Sea (tension)
P25: The answer Italy built is called MOSE, a name taken from Moses, who parted the sea. Construction began in 2003. At each of the three inlets, rows of steel gates lie flat on the seabed, hollow boxes filled with water. There are seventy-eight of them in four barriers: twenty-one and twenty at the Lido inlet, joined by an artificial island built in the middle of the channel, nineteen at Malamocco and eighteen at Chioggia. Each gate is twenty meters wide and up to about twenty-nine meters long.

P26: When a tide above one hundred and ten centimeters is forecast, compressed air pushes the water out of the gates. They swing up on their hinges until they stand above the waves, and the lagoon is shut off from the sea. When the tide falls, water is let back in and the gates sink out of sight.

P27: Getting there was a scandal. In June 2014, police arrested thirty-five people over contracts for the project, among them the mayor of Venice, Giorgio Orsoni, who resigned days later. The final bill was about six billion euros in today's money. All seventy-eight gates were raised together for the first time in a test in July 2020. On the third of October 2020, they went up for a real tide.

P28: Since then, they have gone up again and again: twenty times in the first season, thirteen, nineteen, thirty-two, twenty-three, and then forty-nine times in the season that ended in May 2026, thirty of them in January and February alone. By November 2026 the count had passed one hundred and sixty. On the twenty-second of November 2022, the morning with the one hundred and seventy-three centimeter tide from the start of this video, all four barriers were up, and the city stayed mostly dry. Outside the gates, the gauges at the inlets read one hundred and eighty-seven centimeters at the Lido, one hundred and ninety-one at Chioggia, and two hundred and four at Malamocco. On the city's own table, a tide like that would have flooded around four fifths of Venice.

P29: But every closure cuts the lagoon off from the sea that keeps it alive. The tide is what flushes the lagoon, and ships need the inlets open. As the sea rises, the gates will have to close more often and for longer, perhaps for weeks each year. A barrier built for rare emergencies is turning into a wall that is up more and more often.

## Ending: What Is Left (tension)
P30: How long can this last? A review of Venice's sea-level records puts the likely rise from climate change by 2100 somewhere between eleven centimeters and more than a meter. In April 2026, three climate-adaptation researchers looked at every plan to save the city. They found four paths. Keep MOSE and add new tricks to it. Build ring dikes around the city itself, for up to four and a half billion euros. Seal off the whole lagoon behind a giant levee and pump it day and night, for more than thirty billion, at the cost of the living lagoon. Or, if the sea rises by several meters over the coming centuries, move people inland, for up to a hundred billion.

P31: One of the new tricks would turn the old mistake around. Instead of pumping water out from under Venice, pump seawater back in, deep underground. A 2011 study by Pietro Teatini and his colleagues modeled twelve wells in a ring around the city and found the ground could rise by about twenty-five to thirty centimeters over ten years. Other scientists have warned that it needs far more testing first. If it works, the review found, the gates might hold even after a meter of sea-level rise. After that, the choices get much harder.

P32: Meanwhile, the people are leaving faster than the city sinks. In 1951, one hundred and seventy-four thousand eight hundred and eight people lived in historic Venice. Ten years later it was a hundred and forty-five thousand, by 1971 a hundred and eight thousand, and by 2015 fifty-five thousand. At the end of 2024 the city counted forty-eight thousand four hundred and eighty-nine. Venice may stay above the water. Whether it stays a city is another question.

P33: If you were in charge of Venice, would you keep closing the gates, or seal the lagoon for good and lose what made it Venice?
